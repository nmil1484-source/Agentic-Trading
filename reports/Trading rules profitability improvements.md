# Fix Crypto's Broken Risk Math First

The single highest-leverage, most rigorously supported change available is §21's crypto reward-to-risk floor: at this account's own realized 14.3% crypto win rate (2W/12L), the standard breakeven formula (`p = 1/(1+R)`) and the Kelly criterion independently agree that a 1.3:1 floor sits **below breakeven even under the most statistically optimistic reading of the sample** — the 95% Wilson confidence interval on 2 wins in 14 trades tops out at 39.9%, which itself requires roughly 1.5:1 just to break even, and the 1.3:1 floor currently in force needs 43.5% to break even ([TradingView breakeven win-rate chart](https://vn.tradingview.com/chart/ZC1%21/aJvJwYQK-Winrates-required-to-breakeven-relative-to-stop-target-sizes); [Metricgate, Wilson score interval](https://metricgate.com/blogs/wilson-vs-agresti-coull-vs-clopper-pearson/)). Beyond that single number, five further tightening-only changes are supported with varying strength: redesigning crypto's position-count cap and stop-distance ceiling around documented correlation and volatility realities rather than flat numbers; restructuring Mode B's confirmation-count gate so correlated signals stop masquerading as independent evidence; wiring the system's own profit-factor tracking into a live, automatic size-throttle instead of a passive report; reverting Mode B's per-trade risk back toward its pre-doubling level given streak-survivability math; and — underlying everything else — formally locking the rule set for a minimum number of trades after each change, because the research is unambiguous that 42 trades (and 14 crypto trades, and 1 Mode C trade) are nowhere near enough to distinguish a real edge from noise, a fact the system's own history of reversing the same crypto parameter three times in one day already demonstrates. None of the recommendations below loosen any entry-quality or risk gate; every one tightens a number, closes a measurement gap, or restructures a mechanism to be more discriminating than it is today.

## Crypto's own math says its reward-to-risk floor is upside down

The breakeven relationship between win rate and reward-to-risk is uncontroversial and universally cited: breakeven win rate equals `1/(1+R)`, so a 1.5:1 payoff needs 40% wins, 2:1 needs 33.3%, 3:1 needs 25%, and 4:1 needs 20% ([TradingView](https://vn.tradingview.com/chart/ZC1%21/aJvJwYQK-Winrates-required-to-breakeven-relative-to-stop-target-sizes); [NexusFi, risk/reward ratio](https://nexusfi.com/a/risk-management/risk-reward-ratio)). Applying that formula to the win-rate band crypto is actually producing is not flattering: breakeven R:R at a 30% win rate is ≈2.33:1, at 20% it is 4:1, and at 15% it is ≈5.67:1 — all far above the 1.3:1 floor §21 item 3 currently enforces. This account's crypto sample (2 wins in 14 trades, 14.3%) sits inside that exact band, and because the sample is small, the honest statistical statement is a confidence interval, not a point estimate: the 95% Wilson interval on 2/14 runs from 4.0% to 39.9%. Even generously assuming the true win rate sits at the *top* of that interval — 39.9%, nearly double the observed rate — the breakeven R:R works out to roughly 1.51:1, which the current 1.3:1 floor still fails to clear. At the actual point estimate (14.3%), breakeven R:R is closer to 6:1. The Kelly criterion arrives at the identical verdict through an entirely different mechanism: `f* = W − (1−W)/R`, and plugging in a 14-20% win rate at R:R below roughly 4-5:1 returns a negative fraction — the formula's way of saying no position size is justified at all, let alone the 1.5% of equity §21 item 4 currently risks per trade ([LuxAlgo, Kelly criterion](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/kelly-criterion.md); worked crypto-band calculation in the Kelly research note). Two independent formulas, applied to the account's own measured results, reach the same conclusion from different directions — this is as close to a hard, multiply-corroborated finding as this research produced.

**Recommendation:** raise §21 item 3's crypto reward-to-risk floor from **1.3:1 to at least 2:1 immediately** — a level that clears even the optimistic end of the current confidence interval with real margin, rather than merely matching it — and treat continued escalation toward the 3:1-6:1 range implied by the point estimate as the correct response if win rate does not measurably improve as more trades accumulate. This is **strongly evidence-backed**: the direction (current floor is inadequate) rests on two independently-derived, uncontroversial formulas converging on the same account-specific data; the exact target number (2:1 as an immediate floor, 3-6:1 as a stretch target) is this report's own derived application of that math rather than an externally-published benchmark, so it should be read as rigorously *derived*, not independently *validated* by a second source.

A second, related finding bears directly on sizing. Fractional-Kelly practice — betting a half, quarter, or smaller fraction of the full Kelly number specifically because full Kelly's growth optimum comes with 50-80% drawdowns and severe sensitivity to estimation error — is the near-universal practitioner response to exactly this situation, and multiple sources specifically recommend the *more* conservative end of that range (quarter-Kelly or lower) when the underlying backtest/track record has fewer than ~150 trades ([Kelly Criterion Position Sizing Guide](https://pro.stockalarm.io/blog/kelly-criterion-position-sizing-guide)). With 14 crypto trades, this account is an order of magnitude below that threshold, which means even a theoretically positive Kelly number here would not justify sizing at the full computed fraction. **Recommendation:** rather than set crypto's 1.5% per-trade risk (§21 item 4) as a static number, tie it mechanically to the trailing crypto profit factor the system already tracks in §16 item 16 — halve it to **0.75% while trailing crypto profit factor (last 10 closed trades) sits below 1.0**, restoring to 1.5% only once a subsequent 10-trade window clears 1.0. This is a form of equity-curve-based throttling, a named and documented technique for exactly this situation ([LuxAlgo, equity-curve-based throttling](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/equity-curve-based-throttling.md)) — **moderately evidence-backed**: the technique itself is well-documented and literature-supported, but no source validated this specific 10-trade/0.75%-vs-1.5% threshold, which is this report's own calibration (an explicitly acknowledged gap in the underlying research).

## A flat percentage ceiling and a loose position cap misprice crypto's own volatility and correlation

§21 item 4 applies one flat stop-distance ceiling (15% below entry) across all eight allowlisted coins, from BTC to considerably more volatile smaller-cap alts. The volatility literature is consistent that this is structurally the wrong instrument for a multi-asset basket with heterogeneous volatility: ATR-based (and the related Chandelier Exit) stops are the named, mainstream standard specifically because they scale the stop to each asset's own recently measured range, whereas one flat percentage is either too tight for the more volatile names or uninformatively wide for the calmer ones ([LuxAlgo, five ATR stop-loss strategies](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-atr-stop-loss-strategies-for-risk-control/)). A Cryptohopper illustration makes the mechanism concrete: BTC's own 14-day ATR has run around 2.80%, and BTC closed 2%+ away from the prior close on roughly 29.8% of days in its sample — meaning a flat stop set anywhere near the low end of a "one-size" ceiling gets tripped by BTC's ordinary noise, while the same ceiling may do nothing useful for a smaller, choppier alt with structurally wider normal ranges ([Cryptohopper, stop-losses that survive crypto volatility](https://www.cryptohopper.com/blog/13240-how-to-set-a-stop-loss-that-survives-crypto-volatility)). Notably, Mode C (§20) already uses exactly this ATR-scaled design — entry ±1.5-2×ATR(14), Chandelier-style trailing at 3×ATR — and the research independently validates that choice as the documented standard; crypto's flat-percentage ceiling is the outlier within this system's own rule set, not the ATR approach.

**Recommendation:** replace §21 item 4's flat **15%-of-entry hard stop-distance ceiling with a per-coin ATR(14)-multiple ceiling** (on the order of 2-2.5× ATR(14) as a percentage of entry price, consistent with the 1.5-3× range the ATR literature treats as a reasonable starting band) computed independently for each of the eight allowlisted coins rather than applied as one number across all of them. This is a **restructuring**, not a loosening: for BTC it will likely produce a tighter effective ceiling than 15% in calm periods, and for a genuinely more volatile alt it may permit a wider stop only when that coin's own measured range actually justifies it — it replaces an arbitrary uniform number with one keyed to the asset's own measured behavior. Confidence: **moderate** — the architectural case (ATR scales to measured volatility; a flat percentage across heterogeneous assets is mismatched) is practitioner consensus across multiple independent sources, but no source supplied a rigorous head-to-head backtest quantifying how much this specifically improves outcomes, which is an explicit gap in the literature itself.

The second structural mismatch is §21 item 4's six-of-eight concurrent-crypto-position cap. The correlation literature's consistent principle is to treat a basket of genuinely correlated assets as one larger position for risk-budgeting purposes, and crypto specifically is well-documented to behave this way under stress: following the October 2025 tariff-driven liquidation event, CoinMetrics data showed BTC-ETH correlation rising from 0.69 to 0.73 and BTC-XRP correlation rising from 0.75 to 0.77 over the following eight days — correlations that were already high rose *further* exactly when diversification would have mattered most ([Cointelegraph, without Bitcoin what happens to ether and XRP](https://cointelegraph.com/news/without-bitcoin-what-happens-to-ether-and-xrp)). This is the same dynamic the operating document's own §21 reasoning already names explicitly ("crypto still behaves as a single, highly-correlated macro asset class much of the time") — the cap simply hasn't been set to match that stated belief. Independent sources converge on a 2-6 concurrent-position range as the point past which a "diversified" correlated basket stops providing real diversification benefit ([search-aggregated diversification-concentration sources; cross-referenced against Take Profit's correlation-discount framework](https://takeprofitapp.com/en/learn/correlation-risk-portfolio)), and this same system's own cap started at 2 before six separate same-day raises walked it up to 6.

**Recommendation:** lower §21 item 4's concurrent crypto position cap from **6 back to 2**. This is **strongly evidence-backed in direction** — multiple independent sources on correlated-basket caps, concrete correlation-under-stress data specific to this exact coin set, and the system's own stated rationale all point the same way — though the precise number (2 versus 3) reflects a convergence range across sources rather than one authoritative figure. A complementary, lower-confidence refinement: because the existing 6%-of-equity portfolio heat cap (§3) sums nominal planned risk with no correlation adjustment, consider applying a stress multiplier (one source illustrates ~1.3×) specifically to the summed open risk from simultaneously-held crypto positions before totaling it against the heat ceiling, so the heat cap's math reflects that correlations widen in exactly the selloffs where the cap is supposed to protect the account ([Take Profit, correlation risk in trading portfolios](https://takeprofitapp.com/en/learn/correlation-risk-portfolio)). This last piece is **judgment-based** — the specific multiplier is a single unvalidated heuristic, not a backtested figure, and should be treated as a reasonable adjustment rather than an established standard.

## Mode B's confirmation count treats correlated signals as independent evidence

Mode B's technical-confirmation gate (§5B item 3, and its Bearish Mirror Gate mirror) asks for 1-of-6 confirmations drawn from a pool that includes 9/20 EMA alignment, RSI-above-45-and-improving, and MACD improving — three items that, in a genuinely trending market, will very often move together because they are different mathematical transformations of the same underlying price/momentum series. The entry-gate design literature is consistent and specific about why this is a problem: an N-of-M confirmation count implicitly assumes each signal is an independent draw of evidence, but popular technical indicators are frequently correlated, so "six of seven points may come from similar momentum math" and a nominal pass can really be the same single fact counted multiple times ([MQL5 strategy-design blog](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/774049); [MEXC education](https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/17827791536511)). Traders' Second Brain makes the mechanism explicit — confluence value tracks the *independence* of signals, not their count, and a template with factors labeled under different categories (trend, momentum, volume) "can still double-count the same move" if those categories are empirically correlated regardless of their labels ([Traders' Second Brain, setup confluence factors](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors)). This is especially relevant given that the confirmation floor was lowered from 3-of-6 to 2-of-6 (2026-08-14) and then to 1-of-6 (2026-10-04), each time at explicit user instruction to find more candidates — at 1-of-6, a single correlated momentum reading is now sufficient on its own to "pass," which is the precise failure mode the literature describes.

The converged practitioner alternative is not simply raising the count back up (which this report avoids recommending as a blanket loosened-then-reversed number), but restructuring the pool by the distinct *role* each signal plays — location, state/trend, trigger, volume/execution quality, and risk authority — and requiring independence across roles rather than raw count within one undifferentiated pool, with any genuinely disqualifying condition (no valid stop, inside a timing blackout) kept as a separate hard veto that no score can offset ([LuxAlgo, confluence and scoring systems](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/confluence-and-scoring-systems/); [Traders' Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors)).

**Recommendation:** restructure §5B item 3 (and the Bearish Mirror Gate) so the existing six confirmations are grouped into distinct roles — **Trend/State** (9/20 EMA alignment, price vs. 50-day SMA), **Location** (breakout/retest/range-contraction/Fibonacci pullback), **Relative strength** (vs. SPY/sector), **Volume** (≥1.2x normal or no abnormal selling), and **Momentum** (RSI/MACD) — and require passes from **at least 2 distinct role-groups**, not any 1-of-6 drawn indiscriminately from the pool. Concretely, this blocks what currently qualifies today (e.g., RSI improving alone, or MACD improving alone, both Momentum-role signals) from passing solo, since those are the signals most likely to be correlated restatements of the same trend fact. This functionally tightens the gate relative to the current 1-of-6 standard without simply restoring the pre-2026-10-04 raw count, and it is explicitly a **restructuring**, which the brief permits. Confidence: **moderate** — the architectural critique and the role-based remedy are converged-upon across multiple independent practitioner sources (MQL5, MEXC, LuxAlgo, Traders' Second Brain), but no source supplied a quantified backtest showing exactly how much this specific restructuring improves win rate or profit factor for this kind of system, which the research explicitly flags as an open gap.

A second, lower-confidence but directionally consistent addition for crypto specifically: the academic regime-switching literature gives a principled, non-ad-hoc basis for gating trend-following signals behind a higher-timeframe trend-strength classification, and crypto's well-documented tendency toward extended, multi-month choppy/range-bound stretches (e.g., commentary describing BTC as having moved into a $50,000-$125,000 trading range) is exactly the condition under which un-gated trend signals are expected to produce the most false positives ([Dai, Zhang & Zhu, "Optimal Trend-Following Trading Rules," *Mathematics of Operations Research*, via IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/assmgt/v25y2024i4d10.1057_s41260-024-00357-0.html)). The one quantified claim found for this specific idea — a vendor's assertion that a regime filter raised crypto trend-following win rate from "the 30% range to 55-60%" — is explicitly unverified (no disclosed methodology or sample size), so it should not be treated as established. **Recommendation:** add a standing higher-timeframe (4H/daily) trend-strength or regime gate (e.g., an ADX(14) threshold on the order of 20-25, the most commonly cited retail convention) as an *additional* required condition for crypto entries, layered on top of — never substituting for — the existing confirmation and R:R checks. Confidence: **judgment-based** — strong theoretical/academic grounding for the concept, but the only quantified improvement claim found is a single unverified source, so this should be adopted as a defensible hypothesis to test forward, not a proven fix.

## The system never throttles a sleeve that is demonstrably losing money

§16 item 16 already tracks a running win-rate/profit-factor tally per mode, which is good infrastructure — but nothing in the operating document currently *acts* on that tally beyond narrating it. The portfolio-risk literature's standard answer for exactly this gap is equity-curve-based throttling (sizing a strategy's positions off its own trailing P&L curve, with documented lookback windows of 10-50 closed trades) and performance-based reallocation across sleeves, both of which carry a disclosed, consistent tradeoff: they smooth drawdowns and shorten losing streaks at the cost of giving up some return, because re-engaging lags the strategy's actual turning point in either direction ([LuxAlgo, equity-curve-based throttling](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/equity-curve-based-throttling.md); [What Works in Trading, running multiple strategies](https://whatworksintrading.substack.com/p/my-thoughts-on-how-to-run-multiple)). Given that crypto's combined profit factor sits at 0.23 and Mode B's own profit factor reportedly collapsed from 1.66 to 0.48 coincident with two recent losses, this is precisely the situation this mechanism exists to catch mechanically rather than relying on the user noticing and manually re-tuning rules after the fact — which is what the change log shows happening instead, repeatedly, often multiple times per day.

**Recommendation:** convert §16 item 16's existing tally from passive reporting into an active throttle for every sleeve (Mode B, Mode C, crypto): when a sleeve's trailing profit factor over its last 20 closed trades (10 for the lower-frequency sleeves if 20 hasn't accumulated yet) falls below 1.0, automatically halve that sleeve's per-trade risk percentage and its concurrent-position cap until a subsequent same-length window clears 1.0 again. This is **moderately evidence-backed**: the technique itself is named, documented, and specifically recommended for multi-sleeve retail setups exactly like this one, but no source validated a specific trailing-window length or a 50% cut as the right magnitude — both are this report's own calibration, explicitly filling a gap the research notes themselves flag as unresolved in the literature.

Separately, on Mode B's per-trade risk specifically: the 2026-10-04 change raised §16 item 2 (and the mirrored number in §5B item 3) from 2% to 3% of equity, continuing a string of same-direction raises (1%→2%→3%) made explicitly to "grow faster," not in response to risk evidence. The streak-survivability math in the portfolio-risk literature is directly relevant: capping per-trade risk at 2% means a 5-consecutive-loss streak produces "a manageable drawdown of 10%" ([ICMarkets, cited via search summary](https://www.icmarkets.com/blog/?p=12750)), while a BabyPips forum illustration shows that lower per-trade risk mechanically buys more cushion before any losing streak threatens an aggregate cap — at 1% risk, six consecutive losses are needed to reach the same risk-of-ruin threshold that three losses reach at 2% ([BabyPips forum, choosing position size](https://forums.babypips.com/t/choosing-position-size-1-2-3-4/70644/11)). The portfolio-risk research note's own verdict places 3% "at the high end of what's typically recommended but not outside it," while explicitly noting it narrows the cushion before the account's own circuit breakers (3% intraday decline, 5% weekly loss) trip on fewer consecutive losses than before. **Recommendation:** revert §16 item 2 (and the §5B item 3 cross-reference) from **3% back to 2%** of Agentic Account equity per trade. Confidence: **judgment-based** — the general 1-3% per-trade convention and the streak-survivability math are well-established, but connecting this specific number to Mode B's reported profit-factor decline is this report's own inference from the timing of the rule change, not an independently proven causal link; the decline may be fully or partly attributable to the already-identified "process bug" instead.

## 42 trades cannot tell you which of these numbers is actually broken — lock the rule set before re-tuning again

Every statistical convention found treats a sample this small as, at best, a floor below which no claim should be made at all. Informal practitioner rules of thumb cluster around 20-30 trades as "weak"/"basically useless," ~100 trades as a common threshold for a number starting to mean something, and 200+ as a conservative target for real confidence ([rbtrading.substack.com, "The 200-Trade Rule"](https://rbtrading.substack.com/p/the-200-trade-rule-why-most-traders); Van Tharp Institute's "30 minimum, 100-200 preferred" guidance, cited consistently across secondary sources at [vantharp.com](https://vantharp.com/Weekly_update/Weekly_273_May_31_2006.htm)). The formal statistical version of the same point (Cochran's proportion sample-size formula) says pinning this account's own 14.3% crypto win rate down to even a ±5-point margin at 95% confidence would require roughly 196 trades — nearly fourteen times the 14 actually logged — and the same calculation for the 56% Mode B win rate over 27 trades needs roughly 380. The Wilson intervals computed directly from this account's own numbers make the practical consequence concrete: 14.3% over 14 trades has a 95% CI of **[4.0%, 39.9%]**, and ~56% over 27 trades has a 95% CI of **[37.3%, 72.4%]** — both intervals wide enough to be compatible with a genuinely broken strategy, a genuinely working one, or anything between. A useful proxy for how often a real edge can look fake (or vice versa) over a short window: a Robot Wealth simulation of a strategy with a *true* Sharpe ratio of 1.5 found that an observed result looking strongly unprofitable over a 40-observation window occurs by chance alone roughly 8.5% of the time — not a rare fluke, a routine occurrence ([Robot Wealth, "The Law of Large Numbers, Part 2"](https://robotwealth.com/the-law-of-large-numbers-part-2/)).

This cuts in a specific, important direction for this report: it does not mean "don't tighten anything" — the crypto R:R finding above is robust precisely *because* it holds even at the most optimistic end of the confidence interval, which is a different and stronger kind of claim than "the point estimate looks bad." But it does mean the account's own history of reversing the same parameter multiple times in a single day — the crypto R:R floor moved 1.5:1→1.2:1→1.5:1→1.3:1, and the stop-distance ceiling moved 8%→12%→10%→15%, both within 2026-10-10 alone — is itself the exact failure mode this literature warns against: tuning a rule against noise that a 14-trade sample cannot actually distinguish from signal, then tuning it again a few hours later against the next batch of noise. **Recommendation:** add a standing meta-rule to §12/§14 — a **trade-count lock**: once this report's recommendations (or any future batch of risk/gate-parameter changes) are adopted, no further adjustment to any risk-sizing number, position cap, or confirmation threshold for an affected mode is made until that mode has logged at least **30 new closed trades** (or 20 for crypto, given its materially faster trade cadence) under the revised rules — mirroring the Van Tharp 30-trade floor and the "200 Trade Lock" concept directly. This is **strongly evidence-backed** in its underlying premise (multiple independent, converging minimum-sample-size citations, plus the formal Cochran/Wilson math computed directly on this account's own results), though the specific lock length (30/20 trades) is this report's own translation of that general guidance into an operational number, since no source specifies an exact count for this system's specific trade cadence.

One area deliberately left as a flagged gap rather than a specific recommendation: whether the existing +2R/+3R partial-exit structure (50%/25% scale-out, trailing the remainder) is helping or hurting realized reward-to-risk, especially for crypto's low-win-rate, fat-right-tail-dependent profile. The literature here is explicit that scaling out reliably raises the percentage of trades that close green but has no established effect on *average* expectancy — several sources argue it can structurally cap the exact large winners a low-win-rate trend system depends on ([LuxAlgo, scaling out](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/scaling-out.md); [Trader's Second Brain, take-profit methods](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/take-profit-methods)), but no study was found that tests this specific question for a system like this one. The literature's own consistent advice is to measure it empirically on the system's own trade history rather than assume an answer — and this account's `trades_log.md` already contains the raw data to run that replay. **Recommendation:** before changing the scale-out structure at all, run that replay (recompute what the logged winning trades would have realized under alternative splits) as a measurement step, rather than adjusting the percentages on intuition. Similarly flagged without a specific recommendation: the §18/§19 options and LEAPS premium-based position-size ceilings (12%/6% of equity) have no comparable external benchmark in the retail risk-management literature reviewed — they are structurally different from stop-distance-based risk caps, and no source was found that validates or challenges a specific percentage-of-equity-in-premium convention one way or the other.

## Conclusion

The research converges on a specific, falsifiable story rather than a diffuse "trade less" verdict: crypto's underperformance is not primarily a confirmation-count problem or a position-sizing-formula problem in isolation — it is that the reward-to-risk floor itself is set below the breakeven line implied by the win rate the strategy is actually producing, which is a correctable, well-defined, two-formula-corroborated finding rather than a vague sense that "crypto isn't working." Mode B's decay, by contrast, looks more like a sizing and gate-architecture problem superimposed on a genuinely smaller, noisier sample than the account's own headline profit-factor numbers suggest — the literature's own math shows a 27-trade win-rate estimate has a confidence interval wide enough to be consistent with either a real, durable edge or a lucky early run that is already reverting. The honest takeaway for the system's own change-log culture is that its instinct to tune numbers in response to results is sound in spirit but has been firing on a timescale (sometimes hours) the underlying sample size cannot support — the same discipline that would make any one of these recommendations credible (measuring before changing, locking the rules for a defined number of trades, letting the account's own already-tracked profit-factor tally drive automatic de-risking instead of manual re-litigation) is itself the most durable fix available, independent of which specific number moves where.

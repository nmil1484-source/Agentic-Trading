# Statistical Sample-Size Validity for Trading Strategy Win Rate / Profit Factor

## What minimum sample sizes do practitioners and statisticians cite before trusting a win rate/profit factor?

### Takeaway
There is no single rigorously-derived "magic number" for trading specifically — the formal statistical answer (Cochran's sample-size-for-proportion formula) says the needed count depends on the desired margin of error, confidence level, and how far the true win rate sits from 50%, and it produces numbers ranging from ~100 to ~400+ trades depending on those choices. Informal practitioner rules of thumb cluster around a floor of 20-30 trades (treated as "weak"/"basically useless" on its own), ~100 trades as a common "starting to be meaningful" threshold, and 200-385 trades as a "conservative"/"actionable" target. A sample of 42 trades sits below essentially every cited threshold except the weakest informal floor.

### Cited Findings
- Forum/practitioner consensus (aggregated by search): "30 trades is a weak minimum, around 100 is a common rule of thumb, and 200 to 250 is a conservative target for judging a strategy." One forum post attributes a 20-trade floor to Mark Douglas; another cites 30 trades as "the common rule of thumb for showing some significance." — [futures.io / NexusFi thread](https://futures.io/trading_member/34086-ldp741.html), [StrategyQuant forum](https://strategyquant.com/forum/topic/what-is-your-minimum-number-of-trades-per-year/)
- A blog applying Cochran's sample-size formula (n = Z²·p·q / e²) finds that even a *loose* 70% confidence level with a 5% margin of error requires **~109 trades**, and advises caution below 100 trades. — [Gainium strategy performance metrics blog](https://gainium.io/blog/strategy-performance-metrics)
- "The 200-Trade Rule": a practitioner essay argues a strategy isn't meaningfully tested until ~200 trades; it gives illustrative (not derived/sourced) confidence ranges for a *true* 60% win-rate strategy: at 20 trades the observed win rate could plausibly range 35%-85% ("basically useless"), at 50 trades 46%-74%, and at 200 trades 53%-67% ("actionable"). It recommends 2-3 years of backtesting, ≥50 paper trades, then 200+ combined paper+live trades before full size, and a "200 Trade Lock" (no core-logic changes before reaching 200 trades, since each tweak resets the sample). — [rbtrading.substack.com, "The 200-Trade Rule"](https://rbtrading.substack.com/p/the-200-trade-rule-why-most-traders)
- An agent/vendor backtest-expert guide lists explicit tiers: 30 trades = absolute minimum, 100+ = preferred, 200+ = high confidence; and notes a 5%-per-trade edge needs 100+ trades to distinguish from luck (smaller edges need more). — [agentskills.so backtest-expert guide](https://claudeskills.info/zh/skills/tradermonty/claude-trading-skills/backtest-expert/)
- Van Tharp Institute's own material (cited consistently across multiple secondary sources, though this researcher's direct fetch of the primary page returned HTTP 404 — see Gaps) states you can only get "a good idea" of a system's expectancy with **a minimum of 30 trades**, with **100-200 trades** preferred for a clearer picture. — [Van Tharp Institute Weekly Update #273 (May 31, 2006)](https://vantharp.com/Weekly_update/Weekly_273_May_31_2006.htm) (cited via multiple secondary summaries)
- The `backtrader` Python backtesting library's documentation for its System Quality Number (SQN, a Van-Tharp-derived metric = expectancy / stdev(R) × √N) states the SQN value "should be deemed reliable when the number of trades >= 30," and several secondary sources note the N term in the SQN formula is commonly capped at 100 trades. — [backtrader SQN analyzer docs](https://backtrader.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/analyzers/backtrader.analyzers.sqn.html)
- Profit-factor-specific guidance: one trading-education source says "don't quote your profit factor to anyone (including yourself) until you have at least 100 trades" and explicitly calls out that a profit factor of 4.5 on 20 trades or 4.0 on 12 trades is "statistically meaningless noise," while a profit factor of 1.4 on 300 trades is "more trustworthy" than a high figure on a small set. — [traderssecondbrain.com, "what is good profit factor"](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/what-is-good-profit-factor)
- Quant/academic angle (Sharpe-ratio significance, a related but distinct statistic): a Bayesian-simulation blog post finds that even with 40 trading days of data and a *true* Sharpe ratio of 1.5, a simulated observed Sharpe of ≤ -2 occurs ~8.5% of the time by chance alone, and that sampling uncertainty remains large even after 500 trading days — concluding "even two years of trading might constitute a 'small sample.'" — [Robot Wealth, "The Law of Large Numbers, Part 2"](https://robotwealth.com/the-law-of-large-numbers-part-2/)
- Formal statistical-power framing (no specific trade count given, but the governing formula): required sample size n = 2(z₁₋α/₂ + z₁₋β)² / δ², where δ is the minimum detectable effect size — meaning the "right" n is inherently a function of how small an edge you need to detect, not a fixed number. The same source flags that trading returns are often fat-tailed, violating the normal-distribution assumption behind this formula. — [QuestDB glossary, "Statistical Power Analysis in Backtesting Models"](https://questdb.com/glossary/statistical-power-analysis-in-backtesting-models/)
- Academic (Sharpe-ratio-based) framework for the same underlying question — "how long a track record is needed before you can trust it": Bailey & López de Prado's **Minimum Track Record Length (MinTRL)** gives the number of observations needed to reject the hypothesis that a strategy's true Sharpe ratio is below a benchmark, at a given confidence level; the formula is sensitive to return skewness/kurtosis and the gap between observed and benchmark Sharpe, and "for typical hedge fund return distributions, MinTRL can easily exceed several years of monthly data" per one practitioner summary. — [Bailey & López de Prado, "The Sharpe Ratio Efficient Frontier," *Journal of Risk*](https://www.risk.net/journal-risk/2223785/sharpe-ratio-efficient-frontier); related: [portfoliooptimizer.io summary of Probabilistic Sharpe Ratio / MinTRL](https://portfoliooptimizer.io/blog/the-probabilistic-sharpe-ratio-bias-adjustment-confidence-intervals-hypothesis-testing-and-minimum-track-record-length/); [Bailey & López de Prado, "The Deflated Sharpe Ratio," SSRN 2460551](https://papers.ssrn.com/abstract=2460551)

### Inferences
- The spread of cited numbers (20/30 vs. 100 vs. 200-385+) is not actually a disagreement about the underlying math — it is different authors implicitly choosing different acceptable margins of error and confidence levels inside the same Cochran-style formula. A ±10-point margin at 95% confidence for a ~50% win rate needs ~96-97 trades (the source of most "100 trades" claims); a tighter ±5-point margin at 95% needs ~385 trades (the source of the "200-385" and "hundreds to thousands" claims). A looser 70% confidence / ±5% margin needs only ~109. None of the informal "30 trades" rules of thumb are statistically derived from a stated confidence/margin target — they read as a practical floor below which no claim should be made at all, not a threshold at which confidence is actually achieved.
- Every rule of thumb found (30 / 100 / 200) is substantially larger than 42 total trades, and the strategies with 14 and 1 trades fall below even the weakest cited floor (20-30) by a wide margin.
- The academic Sharpe-ratio/MinTRL literature (Bailey & López de Prado) reinforces the same conclusion from a different statistic: even with far more data (monthly observations over years), confidently distinguishing a real edge from noise is harder than most practitioner rules of thumb imply, especially for non-normal (skewed, fat-tailed) return/R-multiple distributions — which trading P&L series typically are.

### Gaps
- A direct fetch of the Van Tharp Institute's primary page (vantharp.com/Weekly_update/Weekly_273_May_31_2006.htm) returned HTTP 404 in this research session, so the "30 minimum / 100-200 preferred" figures attributed to Van Tharp are corroborated only via consistent secondary characterization (multiple independent search-result summaries and forum citations converging on the same figures and the same URL), not a verified primary-source quote. Flag this to the report writer as secondary-sourced, not primary-verified.
- No peer-reviewed academic paper was found that states a specific minimum trade count for win-rate/profit-factor reliability in the way practitioners frame it (as opposed to Sharpe-ratio-based frameworks like MinTRL, which operate on returns/time rather than discrete trade counts).
- Could not confirm whether QuantStart has published a specific article on this exact topic (minimum trades for backtest significance); a targeted search of quantstart.com specifically returned no matching article in this session.

## How does one compute a confidence interval around an observed win rate for a small sample, and what do the actual intervals look like for 14.3% over 14 trades and ~56% over 27 trades?

### Takeaway
The Wilson score interval is the standard statistically-sound method for a binomial proportion confidence interval on small samples (it stays within [0,1] and has better coverage than the naive/Wald interval, which can even go negative). Computed directly from the standard Wilson formula at 95% confidence: a 14.3% win rate over 14 trades (2 wins) has a 95% CI of roughly **[4.0%, 39.9%]**, and a ~55.6% win rate over 27 trades (15 wins) has a 95% CI of roughly **[37.3%, 72.4%]** — both intervals are wide enough to be compatible with a genuinely losing strategy, a genuinely profitable one, or anything in between.

### Cited Findings
- The Wilson score interval is the standard recommended method for binomial confidence intervals on small samples because the simpler Wald/normal-approximation interval "performs poorly when the sample is small or the proportion is extreme, sometimes even producing bounds below zero or above one," while Wilson stays within the valid [0,1] range and has better coverage; the gap between the two methods is largest when n is small (roughly n < 40). — [Metricgate, "Wilson vs Agresti-Coull vs Clopper-Pearson"](https://metricgate.com/blogs/wilson-vs-agresti-coull-vs-clopper-pearson/)
- Standard Wilson score interval formula (two-sided, confidence level determined by z):
  `center = (p̂ + z²/(2n)) / (1 + z²/n)`
  `margin = [z / (1 + z²/n)] × sqrt( p̂(1-p̂)/n + z²/(4n²) )`
  `CI = center ± margin`
  where p̂ = observed proportion (wins/n), n = sample size, z = the standard-normal critical value for the desired confidence (z ≈ 1.96 for 95%). — general statistical formula, cross-referenced against [Metricgate's Wilson Score Interval calculator/explainer](https://metricgate.com/calculator/wilson-score-interval) and [quantum-algo.com glossary entry on the Wilson score interval](https://www.quantum-algo.com/glossary/wilson-score-interval/)
- **Worked calculation, n=14, x=2 wins (p̂ = 2/14 = 14.3%), z = 1.96 (95% confidence):**
  - z² = 3.8416; denominator (1 + z²/n) = 1 + 3.8416/14 = 1.2744
  - center = (0.1429 + 3.8416/28) / 1.2744 = (0.1429 + 0.1372) / 1.2744 = **0.2197 (21.97%)**
  - variance term: p̂(1-p̂)/n = 0.1429×0.8571/14 = 0.008746; z²/(4n²) = 3.8416/784 = 0.004901; sum = 0.013647; √ = 0.11682
  - margin = (1.96/1.2744) × 0.11682 = 1.5380 × 0.11682 = 0.17977
  - **95% Wilson CI = [0.2197 − 0.1798, 0.2197 + 0.1798] = [4.0%, 39.9%]** — calculated directly from the Wilson formula above by this researcher (cross-checked arithmetic twice).
- **Worked calculation, n=27, x=15 wins (p̂ = 15/27 = 55.6%), z = 1.96 (95% confidence):**
  - z²/n = 3.8416/27 = 0.14228; denominator = 1.14228
  - center = (0.5556 + 3.8416/54) / 1.14228 = (0.5556 + 0.07114) / 1.14228 = **0.5487 (54.9%)**
  - variance term: p̂(1-p̂)/n = 0.5556×0.4444/27 = 0.009145; z²/(4n²) = 3.8416/2916 = 0.001317; sum = 0.010462; √ = 0.10229
  - margin = (1.96/1.14228) × 0.10229 = 1.7159 × 0.10229 = 0.17554... (recomputed precisely: 1.96×0.10229=0.20048; /1.14228... note: formula already divides by denom once inside margin term, so margin = 0.20048/1.14228 = 0.17554 is NOT the method — correct application: margin = (z/denom) × sqrt(...) = (1.96/1.14228) × 0.10229 = 1.7159×0.10229 = 0.17554)
  - **95% Wilson CI = [0.5487 − 0.1755, 0.5487 + 0.1755] ≈ [37.3%, 72.4%]**, matching the direct lower/upper formula calculation: Lower = (0.6267 − 0.2005)/1.14228 = 0.3731 (37.3%); Upper = (0.6267 + 0.2005)/1.14228 = 0.7241 (72.4%).
- **For reference/contrast, n=1, x=1 (a single trade logged as a "win"):** applying the same formula gives a 95% Wilson CI of roughly **[20.6%, 100%]** — i.e., a single trade's observed 100% "win rate" is statistically consistent with a true win rate anywhere from about 1-in-5 to certain. (Calculated directly by this researcher from the same formula; no external source needed for this illustrative extreme case.)
- Context for interpreting these win-rate intervals against a strategy's actual profitability requires comparing the win rate to the *breakeven win rate* implied by its reward:risk ratio: breakeven p = 1/(1+R). E.g., a 1.5:1 reward:risk needs only a ~40% win rate to break even; a 1.3:1 needs ~43.5%. — [Quantum Algo glossary / vendor tooling description of comparing Wilson lower bound to breakeven win rate](https://www.quantum-algo.com/glossary/wilson-score-interval/) (breakeven formula itself is standard risk-reward arithmetic, not exclusive to this source)
- One vendor source's specific worked example (10 wins in 15 trades → ~48% one-sided 90% lower bound) could not be independently reconciled by this researcher's own Wilson calculation (which produced a different number for that scenario), so that particular vendor figure should be treated as unverified/unreliable, not cited as fact.

### Inferences
- Both the 14-trade and 27-trade sub-samples produce 95% confidence intervals roughly 30-40 percentage points wide — far too wide to confirm or rule out any specific "true" win rate, including the breakeven win rate implied by a typical swing-trading reward:risk ratio (~40-43%). For the 14-trade sample, the *entire* plausible range (4.0%-39.9%) sits at or below a ~40% breakeven line, which is suggestive but not dispositive on its own, since a low win rate can still be profitable (profit factor > 1) if the realized reward:risk on winners is large enough — win rate alone, even with a tight interval, cannot establish profit factor; the two must be assessed jointly.
- For the 27-trade sample, the interval (37.3%-72.4%) straddles both sides of essentially every plausible breakeven threshold cited in this document's own reward:risk floors (1.3:1-1.5:1 → ~40-43.5% breakeven) — meaning a 27-trade sample showing a 55.6% observed win rate is consistent with everything from "barely breakeven" to "comfortably profitable," and cannot itself distinguish those cases.
- Applying Cochran's formula (from the prior section) to these same two observed proportions makes the inadequacy concrete: to pin the ~14.3% win rate down to ±5 percentage points at 95% confidence would require roughly n = 1.96²×0.1429×0.8571/0.05² ≈ **196 trades**; to pin the ~55.6% win rate down to ±5 points at 95% confidence would require roughly n = 1.96²×0.5556×0.4444/0.05² ≈ **380 trades**. Both figures are far beyond the 14 and 27 trades actually observed — directly confirming, from first-principles calculation, that neither sub-sample is remotely large enough for its win rate to be treated as a reliable estimate of true edge. (Calculated directly by this researcher using the standard Cochran proportion sample-size formula cited in the prior section.)

### Gaps
- No trading-specific academic or regulatory source was found that applies the Wilson interval (as opposed to generic statistics references and vendor/marketing pages) specifically to trade win rates; the method itself is standard inferential statistics, not something unique to trading, so this is treated as a gap in trading-specific literature rather than a gap in the underlying math.
- The Wilson interval (and the Cochran sample-size formula) assumes trades are independent, identically-distributed Bernoulli trials with a fixed true win probability. No source found directly addresses how much this assumption is violated by autocorrelated/regime-dependent trading results (e.g., clustered wins/losses during a trending vs. choppy regime) or by a strategy whose rules changed mid-sample — both of which would make the true uncertainty even larger than the Wilson interval implies. This should be flagged as a caveat, not resolved.

## What is "trade expectancy" and how many trades does Van Tharp (or similar practitioners) recommend to estimate it reliably?

### Takeaway
Trade expectancy (per Van Tharp's framework) is the average R-multiple (profit or loss expressed as a multiple of initial risk) you can expect per trade, combining win rate and average win/loss size into one number; Tharp's own stated guidance is a minimum of 30 trades to get "a good idea" of a system's expectancy, with 100-200 trades preferred for a clearer, more stable picture.

### Cited Findings
- Expectancy is defined as the average R-multiple per trade: Expectancy = (Win% × Average Win in R) − (Loss% × Average Loss in R), i.e., how many multiples of your initial risk you expect to gain or lose per trade on average, averaged across a system's trade history. — [Two Quants, "R-Multiples and Expectancy"](https://twoquants.substack.com/p/r-multiples-and-expectancy); [traderssecondbrain.com expectancy formula guide](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/expectancy-formula-traders)
- Van Tharp's own published guidance (Van Tharp Institute Weekly Update, cited consistently across multiple secondary sources — see sample-size Gaps above for the primary-fetch caveat): "you can only get a good idea of a system's expectancy with at least 30 trades," and 100 to 200 trades "give a clearer picture." — [Van Tharp Institute Weekly Update #273](https://vantharp.com/Weekly_update/Weekly_273_May_31_2006.htm) (secondary-sourced)
- Van Tharp's related System Quality Number (SQN) metric is explicitly expectancy-based: SQN = (average R-multiple expectancy / standard deviation of R-multiples) × √(number of trades), meaning the metric itself is designed to only become statistically meaningful as trade count grows — one open-source implementation (`backtrader`) explicitly documents that its SQN output "should be deemed reliable when the number of trades >= 30," treating 30 as a floor below which the metric's own output should not be trusted; several secondary sources also note the N term is commonly capped at 100 in the formula (unverified against Tharp's original text). — [backtrader SQN docs](https://backtrader.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/analyzers/backtrader.analyzers.sqn.html); [NexusFi SQN discussion](https://nexusfi.com/a/risk-management/system-quality-number-sqn)
- A non-Tharp quant source (Two Quants) argues more strictly that R-multiple *distributions* (not just the mean expectancy) need to be based on "hundreds, if not thousands" of trades to be meaningful, because the distribution's shape (not just its average) matters for position-sizing decisions. — [Two Quants, "R-Multiples and Expectancy"](https://twoquants.substack.com/p/r-multiples-and-expectancy)

### Inferences
- Even using the most lenient number in Tharp's own framework (30 trades as a bare floor for "a good idea" of expectancy, not a reliable estimate), a 14-trade or a 1-trade sub-strategy sample falls short, and even the 27-trade sample falls just short of that minimum floor. None of the three sub-samples in the scenario under review (42 total trades split 14/27/1) reaches Tharp's own preferred 100-200 trade range for a "clearer picture" of expectancy.
- The SQN formula's explicit √N scaling term is a direct, quantitative illustration of why small samples are untrustworthy under this exact framework: all else equal, a system's SQN score (its own stated measure of statistical confidence in the result) is mechanically smaller — i.e., less statistically convincing — at n=14 or n=27 than it would be at n=100+, independent of whatever the raw win-rate/expectancy numbers show.

### Gaps
- Tharp's original book-length treatment (e.g., *Trade Your Way to Financial Freedom* or *The Definitive Guide to Position Sizing Strategies*) was not directly accessed in this research session — only secondary summaries and one weekly-update page (which returned a 404 on direct fetch) were available. The 30/100-200 figures should be treated as well-corroborated by consistent secondary citation, but not primary-source-verified by this researcher.

## Are there known examples of a strategy showing a seemingly negative edge over a small sample before reverting to a documented positive edge over a larger sample, or vice versa — how common is this "small-sample noise" in practice?

### Takeaway
No source found provides a concrete, named, verifiable case study (e.g., "Strategy X looked like a -0.5 profit factor over its first 15 trades, then reverted to +1.3 over 300 trades") with real published numbers; the available literature instead establishes the phenomenon theoretically/statistically (via coin-flip analogies, simulation, and related hedge-fund-performance-persistence research) and confirms it is a well-understood, expected form of noise — just not documented in this search as a single named trading-strategy anecdote with hard before/after data.

### Cited Findings
- The "200-Trade Rule" essay gives an illustrative (not verified/real) hypothetical: a strategy with a backtested 65% true win rate went 4-6 (40% win rate) in its first 10 live trades and was down 3%, which the author argues is explainable as ordinary variance rather than a broken strategy — but the piece explicitly presents this as a hypothetical example, not documented real results, and gives no source for its probability claims. — [rbtrading.substack.com, "The 200-Trade Rule"](https://rbtrading.substack.com/p/the-200-trade-rule-why-most-traders)
- The same essay separately asserts (again without a stated derivation) that a strategy with a true 65% win rate will show 6 or more losses in a 10-trade window "about 6% of the time," and that a true 60%-win-rate strategy has roughly a 1.02% chance of 5 consecutive losses in any given 5-trade sequence — illustrating that losing streaks that look alarming over a dozen-or-so trades are not actually rare events for a genuinely profitable system. — [rbtrading.substack.com, "The 200-Trade Rule"](https://rbtrading.substack.com/p/the-200-trade-rule-why-most-traders)
- Closest real (non-hypothetical) evidence found of performance reversal over time at the strategy/fund level, though not matched to the specific "dozen-plus trades" scale requested: Kat & Palaro's study (summarized by CXO Advisory) comparing live hedge funds to mechanical replicating strategies found that "aggregate performance of individual hedge funds deteriorates substantially over time, with a tendency for performance reversal on a fund-by-fund basis" — i.e., documented empirical regression-to-the-mean in realized fund performance, though measured in months/years of fund returns, not discrete trade counts. — [CXO Advisory summary of Kat & Palaro](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=1128)
- A 2026 arXiv study examining 1,726 rules-based strategies from ten institutions (2009-2025) found live trading performance is systematically weaker than the backtests used to market those strategies, and notes that the measured gap is likely a *lower bound* on the true backtest-to-live decay across an unselected universe of strategies (since strategies that were tested but never launched aren't part of the observed sample) — relevant context for why early live results can look different from a strategy's "true" edge, though this is about backtest-vs-live divergence generally, not small-sample noise specifically. — [arXiv:2604.18821, "Evaluating Structured Strategy Backtests"](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2604.18821)
- The Robot Wealth Sharpe-ratio simulation (cited above) is the most concrete quantitative illustration found of the general phenomenon: with a genuinely profitable system (true Sharpe = 1.5), an observed Sharpe ratio of ≤ -2 (i.e., looking strongly unprofitable) over a 40-trading-day window occurs by chance alone roughly 8.5% of the time — a non-trivial, "roughly 1-in-12" probability of a real edge looking like a clear negative edge over a short sample purely from statistical noise. — [Robot Wealth, "The Law of Large Numbers, Part 2"](https://robotwealth.com/the-law-of-large-numbers-part-2/)
- General framing (coin-flip analogy, repeated across multiple sources): a small sample (e.g., 10-20 trials) from a process with a real, modest edge is statistically indistinguishable from pure noise, while the same edge becomes "unmistakable" over thousands of trials — i.e., both false "it's broken" and false "it's working" conclusions are expected and common at small-N, not aberrations. — [search-aggregated across multiple sources discussing the law of large numbers in trading contexts, e.g. concepts.dsebastien.net and slopeofhope.com commentary]

### Inferences
- The absence of a single, named, "strategy A looked negative over N trades then proved positive over M trades" case study with hard published numbers is itself informative: this kind of reversal is treated in the literature as a statistically *expected*, almost definitionally unremarkable outcome of small-sample variance (hence illustrated with coin-flip/simulation analogies rather than documented as a notable real-world anecdote) — it is not a rare or surprising event that would warrant its own case study the way, say, a flash crash would.
- The Robot Wealth ~8.5% figure, while computed for Sharpe ratio rather than win rate/profit factor directly, is a useful proxy for the scenario in question: it quantifies that a genuinely good strategy showing clearly bad short-sample results is not a rare edge case but a routinely-occurring outcome (roughly 1 in 12 short windows), directly supporting the inference that a 14-trade or 27-trade sub-strategy's results, taken alone, have a meaningful chance of misrepresenting the sign of its true edge in either direction.

### Gaps
- No verifiable, named, real-world case study with concrete before/after trade-level numbers (as opposed to hypothetical illustrations or fund-level aggregate statistics measured in time rather than trade count) was found for the specific "dozen-plus trades negative, then larger sample positive (or vice versa)" scenario. This should be reported to the report-writer as a genuine gap in the available public literature, not papered over with a hypothetical presented as real. If a stronger case study is needed, likely next steps would be: (a) searching academic finance journals directly for "performance reversal" + "trading strategy" + "small sample," or (b) running an original Monte Carlo simulation (e.g., a biased-coin / fixed-true-edge simulation) to generate a reproducible, numerically concrete illustration in place of a real-world anecdote.

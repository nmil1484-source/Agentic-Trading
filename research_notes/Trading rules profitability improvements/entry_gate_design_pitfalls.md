# Entry-Gate Design Pitfalls: Confirmation-Count Checklists vs. Alternatives

## Is there documented critique of "checklist"/confirmation-count entry systems (count N-of-6 signals, require a minimum count)? What specifically goes wrong?

### Takeaway
The critique found is consistent and clear but comes almost entirely from practitioner/vendor sources rather than peer-reviewed academic work: counting how many of N indicators are "true" systematically overstates confidence because most popular technical indicators are mathematically correlated (derived from the same price series via similar transformations), so a checklist treats redundant restatements of the same signal as independent corroboration. No source found directly validates or invalidates a specific "N-of-6" design with a controlled backtest; the critique is about the logical/statistical flaw in the architecture, not an empirically-measured performance gap.

### Cited Findings
- An MQL5 strategy-design article describes a points-per-indicator scoring approach and notes that "six of seven points may come from similar momentum math" — i.e., a 6-of-7 "pass" can really be one piece of evidence counted six times — [MQL5 blog](https://www.mql5.com/en/blogs/post/774049)
- A MEXC educational article makes the same point using MACD and an EMA crossover specifically, which "often signal the same thing" despite being counted as two separate confirmations — [MEXC](https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/17827791536511)
- Traders' Second Brain argues explicitly that confluence value tracks *signal independence*, not signal count, giving an example where two genuinely uncorrelated signals outweigh six signals that are highly correlated with each other — [Traders' Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors)
- The same source states the mechanism plainly: "three indicators derived from the same price move may look like three confirmations" while carrying nearly the same information, which "inflates apparent confidence" — [Traders' Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors)
- The same source warns that *category labels* (e.g., labeling one indicator "momentum," another "volume") can hide correlation — a template with one price factor, one momentum factor, and one volume factor "can still double-count the same move" if those factors are empirically correlated regardless of their category label — [Traders' Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors)
- A second MEXC point: with 6-7 indicators available, a trader who already wants to take the trade can usually find enough bullish readings to justify it, and contradicting signals "get explained away" — i.e., confirmation-count systems are also vulnerable to confirmation bias in which signals get selectively attended to — [MEXC](https://www.mexc.com/learn/article/17827791536511)
- LuxAlgo's confluence/scoring concept guide, describing equal-weight voting ("how many of N conditions are true") as a common architecture, states plainly: "more confluence doesn't automatically mean a higher win rate," and "a high score raises the evidence bar; it does not guarantee the outcome" — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/confluence-and-scoring-systems/)
- LuxAlgo also notes the selectivity trade-off is real: requiring more conditions to agree mechanically cuts trade frequency (fewer trades pass a stricter N-of-M bar), independent of whether those trades are actually better — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/confluence-and-scoring-systems/)
- A TradingView "Confluence Signal Engine" script description explicitly labels the failure mode "correlated noise presented as agreement" when multiple indicators derived from similar inputs are stacked and counted as separate confirmations — cited secondhand via a vendor search (original script page not independently fetched; treat as a single vendor's framing, not verified independently)

### Inferences
- The core statistical flaw across all these sources is the same: an N-of-M confirmation-count gate implicitly assumes each of the M signals is an *independent* draw of evidence, when in practice many popular technical indicators (EMA alignment, MACD, RSI, price-vs-SMA, volume) are different transformations of the same underlying price/volume series over overlapping lookback windows, so their pass/fail outcomes are correlated — the gate's true "effective N" of independent evidence is smaller than its nominal count, meaning a "4-of-6" pass can carry roughly the same information as "2-of-3" would if the three were the genuinely independent ones.
- A second, distinct flaw raised across sources: binary pass/fail per indicator discards signal *strength* — a technical confirmation that is "just barely" true (e.g., RSI at 45.1, the exact floor) counts identically to one that is strongly true (RSI at 70), so two setups with the same confirmation count can have very different underlying conviction. None of the sources found this framed as "strength blindness" explicitly, but it follows directly from the binary architecture they critique and is the natural complement to the independence critique.
- No source quantifies "how much worse" a correlated-indicator checklist performs versus an independence-aware or weighted design in a controlled, out-of-sample backtest — the critique in the literature found is architectural/statistical reasoning, not a measured effect size. A system relying on an N-of-M confirmation count (as in a document specifying "1 of 6" or "2 of 6" technical confirmations) should treat its "pass" as weaker evidence than its face-value count suggests, especially when confirmations are frequently correlated in practice (e.g., "9/20 EMA bullish alignment" and "RSI above 45 and improving" will very often move together in a trending market).

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed academic paper was found specifically critiquing "N-of-M confirmation count" entry systems in algorithmic/systematic trading; all critique found is from trading-education blogs, vendor documentation, and TradingView script descriptions, not academic literature.
- Could not verify Ernest Chan's specific published views on combining correlated technical indicators — search results returned only generic, likely-unreliable mirror-site summaries of his book *Quantitative Trading*, not his actual text or a reliable secondary source quoting him directly on this topic.
- Could not verify Perry Kaufman's (*Trading Systems and Methods*) specific text on indicator-combination risk; one third-party profile claims he combines five trend detectors with two confirmation indicators, but this could not be confirmed against the primary source.
- No source was found that empirically measured the "effective N" (degree of correlation) among a specific common basket of swing-trading indicators (9/20 EMA, 50-SMA, breakout/retest, relative strength, volume, RSI/MACD) — the kind of basket many N-of-6 checklist systems actually use.

## What do practitioners recommend instead of simple N-of-M counting (weighted/scored confluence, regime filters, volatility-adjusted sizing/stops, ML-scored quality)?

### Takeaway
Three concrete, broadly-converged alternatives emerged from practitioner sources: (1) weighted/scored confluence with hard veto conditions that override the score, (2) treating correlation/independence as a design constraint — grouping signals by the distinct *role* they play (location, state, trigger, execution quality, risk authority) rather than by superficial indicator category, and (3) validating any weighting scheme out-of-sample rather than assigning weights by intuition. ML-scored setup quality exists as a product category but has no independent evidence of outperforming rule-based scoring; it is a niche/unproven approach, not a mainstream practitioner consensus.

### Cited Findings
- LuxAlgo's framework for alternatives to simple equal-weight voting includes: **weighted scoring** (factors scaled by assumed importance, with equal weights described as "the robust default" and the warning that "intuition-based or single-backtest weights add overfitting risk"); **A+ grading** (setups ranked by how many criteria they meet, with position size scaling by grade rather than binary pass/fail); and **veto conditions** (a single disqualifier overrides any score entirely — examples given: a scheduled macro event, trading against the higher-timeframe trend, an excessively wide spread, or a conflicting correlated open position) — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/confluence-and-scoring-systems/)
- LuxAlgo's independence requirements for building a scoring system: choose factors from genuinely different dimensions (trend, momentum, volatility, location-relative-to-a-level, volume); explicitly reject a candidate factor that mostly correlates with factors already on the list; and validate the score out-of-sample, confirming higher scores actually preceded better outcomes, pruning factors that add correlation without adding information — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/confluence-and-scoring-systems/)
- Traders' Second Brain's "role-based" alternative to category-based confirmation: assign each factor a distinct job — **location** (where price is), **state** (trend/momentum condition), **trigger** (what times the entry), **execution quality** (spread/liquidity), and **risk authority** (what sets/invalidates the stop) — each factor must contribute non-overlapping information, and independence should be tested both semantically (compare each factor's inputs/transformation/timeframe) and empirically (cross-tabulate how often factors' pass/fail states coincide across all historical candidates, including rejected ones) — [Traders' Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors)
- The same source recommends measuring each factor's *incremental value* directly: remove one factor at a time and check whether performance (after costs, on untouched/out-of-sample data) actually improves or degrades without it — a factor that doesn't change outcomes when removed is not adding real information regardless of how its category label reads — [Traders' Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors)
- Both sources agree hard gates should stay separate from any score: a failed required condition (e.g., no valid stop, inside a timing blackout) should block the trade outright and must not be "offset" by high scores elsewhere in the checklist — [Traders' Second Brain](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/setup-confluence-factors); [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/confluence-and-scoring-systems/)
- On regime filters specifically (see next section for detail): LuxAlgo separately documents "regime-adjusted" designs where a volatility or trend-strength regime classification changes which rules fire or how wide stops/lookbacks are set, while warning that "the extra thresholds can be overfit" — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/volatility-regime-switches/)
- On ML-scored setup quality: one commercial TradingView indicator claims to weigh "setup context, trigger-bar strength, market regime, and how similar past setups performed" and output a followthrough-probability percentage trained on actual price outcomes — but this is a single vendor's unverified claim, not independently evaluated, and the vendor's own disclaimer states "no rule-based tool can know future price behavior, order-book conditions, spreads, or a user's execution plan" — [TradingView/vendor search result, unverified]
- A broader academic ensemble-methods search (not ML-scoring specifically, but adjacent) found a 2025 arXiv paper on tabular deep learning for equity signal generation reporting that a hybrid ensemble was the *only* model variant with statistically significant out-of-sample alpha, attributed explicitly to the component models' errors being "weakly correlated" — reinforcing the independence principle (weak correlation between signal sources, not raw count of sources, is what adds value) even in an ML context — [arXiv](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2608.27076) (not independently verified beyond the search summary; full paper not fetched)
- A glossary source on ensemble models states plainly that "combining highly correlated models adds little benefit," the identical principle as the indicator-checklist critique above, applied to ML model combination — [Wright Research glossary, via search summary]

### Inferences
- Across both the indicator-checklist critique and the ML-ensemble literature, the single unifying practitioner-recommended principle is: **value comes from the independence of evidence sources, not their count** — whether the "votes" are technical indicators or trained models. Any entry-gate design (rule-based or ML) that adds more signals without checking pairwise/cross-tab correlation among them is vulnerable to the same inflation-of-confidence problem.
- Weighted scoring and veto conditions are **widely-converged, mainstream-practitioner recommendations** (found consistently across multiple independent sources: LuxAlgo, Traders' Second Brain, MQL5, generic "A+ grading" concept); ML-scored setup quality is a **niche/unverified** approach — it exists as a product category but no source found provides independent evidence it beats a well-constructed rule-based score, and it carries its own distinct risk (overfitting to historical outcome labels) that is arguably harder to audit than a transparent rule-based checklist.
- A practical implication for a rule-based system that wants to keep its checklist architecture but reduce the correlated-redundancy problem: group existing confirmations into the role categories above (location / state / trigger / execution-quality / risk-authority) and require at least one pass from more than one role-category, rather than any N count drawn indiscriminately from a single pool where several items may be restatements of the same trend/momentum fact.

### Gaps
- No source quantified exactly how much a weighted/vetoed scoring system outperforms a simple N-of-M count in a controlled backtest on the same instrument universe — the recommendation is grounded in statistical reasoning (independence, overfitting risk of ad hoc weights) rather than a demonstrated effect size.
- Could not independently verify the TradingView ML-scoring vendor's claims about its training methodology or any backtested performance; treat as an unverified product claim, not evidence that ML scoring is validated or recommended by the broader practitioner community.
- No academic literature on "technical analysis signal combination" (e.g., formal studies on combining RSI/MACD/EMA signals with correlation-aware weighting) was located in the searches performed; this appears to be an under-studied area in the formal literature relative to how common checklist-style systems are in retail/semi-systematic trading.

## Specifically on volatility-adjusted stops: how does the standard ATR-multiple approach compare to a flat percentage-based stop-distance ceiling for assets with varying volatility (e.g., different cryptocurrencies)?

### Takeaway
ATR-based stops (typically entry ± 1.5-3x ATR(14), with the Chandelier Exit's 22-period/3x-ATR trailing variant being a well-documented, named mainstream standard attributed to Chuck LeBeau and popularized by Alexander Elder) are the broadly-accepted standard specifically *because* they scale the stop distance to each asset's own recently-measured volatility, whereas a single flat-percentage ceiling applied uniformly across assets of very different volatility (e.g., BTC vs. a more volatile small-cap altcoin) either cuts off technically valid setups on the more volatile asset or is too wide to meaningfully protect the less volatile one. No source found provided a rigorous backtest numerically comparing the two approaches head-to-head; the comparison found in the literature is conceptual/practitioner consensus, not an empirically measured performance delta.

### Cited Findings
- The Chandelier Exit is a trailing stop that measures from the highest high (for longs) or lowest low (for shorts) over a rolling lookback window (not from the entry price), offset by an ATR multiple; it was developed by Chuck LeBeau and popularized by Alexander Elder in his 2002 book *Come Into My Trading Room* — [via TradingView/Incredible Charts search summary]
- Typical/default Chandelier Exit parameters are a 22-bar lookback and a 3x ATR multiplier; some sources describe a 2.5x-3.5x multiplier range as common, with a higher multiplier widening the stop and a lower multiplier tightening it — [via search summary of TradingView/Incredible Charts sources]
- LuxAlgo's dedicated ATR stop-loss article describes five distinct ATR-based stop designs: (1) a basic static ATR stop (entry ± multiplier × ATR-at-entry), (2) an ATR trailing stop (measured from the best price reached since entry, only ever tightening — "a stop should not loosen after it moves in the trade's favor"), (3) the Chandelier Exit variant (measured from the rolling highest-high/lowest-low, not since entry; StockCharts' defaults cited as 22 periods / 3x multiplier), (4) an ATR-percentage stop (ATRP, expressing ATR as a % of price to allow cross-instrument volatility comparison), and (5) a regime-adjusted ATR stop (multiplier changes when a volatility regime condition is met, relative to a historical baseline) — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-atr-stop-loss-strategies-for-risk-control/)
- The same article explicitly states "there is no universal best multiplier" and treats 1x/2x/3x as test parameters to be backtested per-instrument rather than fixed recommendations, with 14 periods cited as "a common ATR starting point" that should still be tested — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-atr-stop-loss-strategies-for-risk-control/)
- A separate practitioner comparison (via search summary, source not independently fetched) states a fixed percentage stop is "the simplest method, with predictable per-trade risk, but ignores volatility and structure" — a 2% stop is "the same distance in a quiet market and a volatile one" — while an ATR multiple "widens and tightens with measured conditions," generally placing the exit "more sensibly relative to noise," and that ATR stops "perform better when volatility regimes shift" — [via search summary, LuxAlgo "ATR-based Stop Distance" concept page, URL in search result but not independently re-fetched]
- A Cryptohopper blog post reports a specific illustrative data point: BTC/USDT's 14-period daily ATR on Binance was measured at 2.80%, and BTC closed 2% or more away from the prior close on 29.8% of days in its sample period; its stated conclusion is that a flat 2% stop is frequently tripped by BTC's ordinary day-to-day movement rather than a genuine trend reversal — [Cryptohopper](https://www.cryptohopper.com/blog/13240-how-to-set-a-stop-loss-that-survives-crypto-volatility) (vendor blog, single illustrative sample — not an independently audited statistic, and the sample period/methodology were not disclosed in the search summary)
- The same source area notes practical guidance that stop width should vary by liquidity/volatility tier within crypto itself — wider stops for low-volume altcoins, tighter stops for high-liquidity pairs — directly illustrating why one flat percentage ceiling applied uniformly across a basket of named coins (e.g., BTC through smaller-cap, more volatile alts) would be miscalibrated for at least some of them — [via search summary, Cryptohopper/Alphaex Capital sources]
- Common ATR parameter guidance converges on roughly 14-period ATR with a 1.5x-3x multiplier across multiple sources, though all explicitly caveat this as a starting point requiring instrument-specific testing, not a universal constant — [LuxAlgo](https://www.luxalgo.com/blog/5-atr-stop-loss-strategies-for-risk-control/); [via search summary of additional crypto-stop guides]

### Inferences
- A **fixed stop-distance ceiling expressed as one flat percentage across a multi-asset list with heterogeneous volatility** (e.g., applying the same maximum-stop-distance percentage to BTC and to a smaller, more volatile altcoin) is structurally mismatched to each asset's actual noise level: it will tend to be too tight for the more volatile asset (triggering on ordinary noise before the thesis is actually invalidated, per the Cryptohopper BTC illustration above) and unnecessarily loose — or simply uninformative about risk — for the least volatile asset in the basket.
- The ATR-based alternative directly addresses this by sizing the stop to each instrument's own recently-measured range rather than a single number applied uniformly; this is consistent with why ATR-based and Chandelier-style stops are the named, widely-cited mainstream standard in both the general trading literature and (per the LuxAlgo/Cryptohopper sources specifically addressing crypto) explicitly recommended as more appropriate than flat percentages for crypto's high and uneven cross-asset volatility.
- The "regime-adjusted ATR stop" (LuxAlgo's fifth variant) is a further refinement — scaling the ATR multiplier itself up or down depending on whether current volatility is elevated or depressed relative to its own historical baseline — which goes a step beyond a static ATR multiple and could be relevant for crypto given its documented history of alternating low-volatility range-bound stretches and high-volatility breakout/crash periods (see next section's findings on crypto regime behavior).
- No source made a direct, numerically-backed claim such as "ATR stops improve returns/drawdown by X% versus flat-percentage stops" — the consensus found is architectural/conceptual (ATR stops are "more sensibly" calibrated to noise) rather than a demonstrated quantitative edge. A system choosing between the two should treat "ATR-based is structurally more appropriate for a multi-asset, variable-volatility universe" as well-supported practitioner consensus, but should not cite a specific performance-improvement number as proven, since none was found.

### Gaps
- No rigorous head-to-head backtest (same strategy, same instrument universe, ATR-stop variant vs. flat-percentage-stop variant, compared on win rate/drawdown/expectancy after costs) was located in the sources searched.
- The Cryptohopper 2.80%-ATR/29.8%-of-days statistic is a single vendor blog's illustrative sample with no disclosed date range or methodology; it should be treated as directional/illustrative only, not a rigorously verified statistic.
- Turtle Trader-specific ATR-multiple stop/sizing rules (explicitly requested as a suggested source) could not be located in these searches — results returned only Chandelier Exit and general ATR material, with no hit on the original Turtle Trading "N" (ATR-based unit) methodology; a more targeted follow-up search specifically on "Turtle Trading N ATR position sizing Richard Dennis" would be needed to close this gap.
- No source gave a specific, tested ATR-multiplier recommendation differentiated by named cryptocurrency (e.g., "use 2x for BTC but 3x for a smaller-cap altcoin") — only general liquidity-tier guidance (wider for low-volume alts, tighter for high-liquidity pairs) without specific numbers.

## What do people mean by "regime filter" in a trend-following context, and is there evidence it measurably improves win rate or reduces false signals, especially in crypto's extended range-bound/choppy periods?

### Takeaway
A "regime filter" in trend-following means gating entries (or switching rule-sets/parameters entirely) based on a higher-level classification of the market's current state — most commonly trend-strength (e.g., ADX above/below a threshold distinguishing "trending" from "choppy") or a bull/bear probability estimated from a formal regime-switching model — so that trend-following rules only fire when the regime itself supports them. There is real academic literature establishing the formal basis for this (regime-switching models demonstrably reproduce known market behaviors and can specify theoretically optimal trend-following entry/exit thresholds conditional on regime), and there is a rich literature establishing *why* it should help (trend-following's structurally low ~30-45% win rate is itself evidence that un-gated trend signals fire often in regimes that don't support them) — but rigorous, quantified backtest evidence that a regime filter measurably improves win rate or reduces false signals *specifically in crypto* was not found; what exists is strong conceptual/theoretical support plus one unverified practitioner claim of a large win-rate improvement.

### Cited Findings
- Trend-following strategies characteristically have **low win rates, typically cited around 30-45%**, with profitability instead coming from a small number of large winners offsetting many small losses (the "high payoff ratio, low win rate" shape) — [via search summary, NexusFi/general trend-following sources]
- One explicit trade-off statement found: "in order to deliver a high payoff ratio they must sacrifice a high win rate. If you try to increase the fraction of winning trades, the payoff ratio will suffer" — i.e., filtering to raise win rate is not free; it is expected to come with fewer and/or smaller winners unless the filter specifically removes *false* signals rather than *valid-but-smaller* ones — [via search summary, trend-following source, exact origin unclear from the search snippet]
- On the academic/formal side: Dai, Zhang, and Zhu's paper "Optimal Trend-Following Trading Rules" (published in *Mathematics of Operations Research*, 2016) derives entry/exit threshold curves for trend-following conditional on an estimated probability of being in a bull market, under a formal regime-switching model — i.e., the academic literature explicitly frames "regime probability" as the correct conditioning variable for trend-following entries, not an ad hoc filter — [IDEAS/RePEc](https://ideas.repec.org/a/pal/assmgt/v25y2024i4d10.1057_s41260-024-00357-0.html); original at [WMU ScholarWorks](https://scholarworks.wmich.edu/math_pubs/38)
- A follow-on 2025 academic chapter (Zakamulin and Giner) extends this to derive optimal trend-following rules for two-state (bull/bear) regimes under both Markov and semi-Markov regime-switching processes, explicitly incorporating transaction costs into the optimal rule — [EconPapers](https://econpapers.repec.org/bookchap/sprsprchp/978-3-031-90907-8_5f12.htm)
- Alpha Architect's practitioner summary of this academic line states that most *existing* (ad hoc, non-regime-aware) trend rules are just that — ad hoc — and that a formal two-state regime-switching process can reproduce known stylized facts of real markets such as fat tails and short-term momentum, giving the regime-based approach a stronger theoretical foundation than fixed-threshold indicator rules — [search summary; original article returned HTTP 403 on direct fetch and could not be independently verified beyond the search engine's summary]
- The most common practical regime filter in retail/semi-systematic use is **ADX-threshold-based**: ADX above ~25 is commonly treated as "trending" and below ~20 as "choppy/non-directional," an idea attributed (per a Barchart educational article) to J. Welles Wilder, ADX's creator — [Barchart, via search summary](https://www.barchart.com/story/news/34902159/use-adx-to-find-stronger-trade-setups-and-avoid-getting-whipsawed-by-weak-stock-trends)
- That same Barchart source presents the ADX-filter claim ("filtering for ADX above 20-25 reduces whipsaws") as a rule-of-thumb/design rationale, explicitly **without backtest numbers** — a second ADX-filter search specifically looking for backtest evidence likewise found "no peer-reviewed or out-of-sample evidence" and multiple vendor/TradingView sources asserting whipsaw-reduction as a design rationale rather than a measured result
- One specific, unverified practitioner claim was found: a "Trading Research Hub (Pedma)" article claims a filtering method raised the win rate of binary crypto trend-following "from the 30% range to 55-60%" while "maintaining the core trend-following approach" — this could not be independently verified (methodology, sample size, and whether total *returns* improved — as opposed to just win rate — were not disclosed in the search summary, and raising win rate alone does not establish improved expectancy if average winner size fell) — treat as an unverified, single-source claim, not established evidence
- On crypto's own choppy/range-bound behavior specifically: multiple 2022-2026 market-commentary sources (Cointelegraph, Bloomberg, Brooks Trading) describe Bitcoin repeatedly entering extended, multi-month trading ranges with "whipsaws" (price moving one way, then reversing quickly) rather than sustained, singly-directional trends — e.g., a 2026-dated Brooks analysis described BTC as having "transitioned from a long-term bull trend into a major trading range between $50,000 and $125,000" — [via search summary, Cointelegraph/Bloomberg/Brooks Trading Course sources]
- These crypto-commentary sources establish that extended choppy/range-bound regimes are a real, recurring, and currently-relevant feature of BTC's price behavior, which is the exact environment in which un-gated trend-following signals are expected (per the general trend-following literature) to generate the most false/losing signals — but none of these market-commentary sources themselves test or quantify a regime filter's effect on a trading system's results; they are price-history commentary, not strategy backtests

### Inferences
- The strongest, most defensible claim supportable from this research is: **regime filtering has a sound theoretical/academic basis (formal regime-switching models provide a principled, non-ad-hoc conditioning variable for trend-following rules) and a plausible mechanism for improving signal quality (crypto specifically has well-documented, extended choppy/range-bound stretches that are exactly the conditions where trend-following checklists are expected to produce false positives) — but the specific, quantified claim "a regime filter improves win rate by X points" or "reduces false signals by Y%" is not well-evidenced for crypto in the sources found.** The one specific number found (30% → 55-60% win rate) is a single unverified vendor/blog claim that should not be treated as established fact.
- There is an important caveat raised independently by two different sources: raising win rate via a stricter filter is not automatically a net improvement, because trend-following's edge structurally depends on a *low* win rate paired with a high payoff ratio — a filter that simply removes trades indiscriminately (rather than specifically removing false/low-quality signals) could reduce trade count and payoff ratio together, netting out to no real improvement in expectancy. Any regime-filter addition to a trading system should be evaluated on expectancy/profit-factor after the fact, not on win rate alone — which is directly consistent with language already present in trading-system documentation that tracks profit factor specifically because "win rate alone is misleading."
- Given crypto's documented tendency toward extended range-bound regimes, a plausible, literature-consistent design would gate crypto trend-following entries behind a higher-timeframe trend-strength or regime classification (e.g., ADX on a daily/4H timeframe, or a longer moving-average slope/regime check) in addition to — not instead of — the existing confirmation-count and R:R checks, specifically to avoid taking "1-of-6 confirmation" trend trades inside a range where the higher-timeframe context does not support sustained directional movement. This is inference, not a direct citation, since no source tested this exact combination.

### Gaps
- No rigorous, verifiable backtest was found quantifying a regime filter's effect on win rate, false-signal rate, or (most importantly) profit factor/expectancy specifically for a crypto trend-following system; the one specific number found (30%→55-60% win rate) is unverified and its methodology/sample size/return impact are unknown.
- Could not independently fetch Alpha Architect's regime-switching article (returned HTTP 403) to verify its characterization of the academic literature beyond the search engine's own summary — treat that summary as secondhand.
- No source directly tested ADX (or any other regime classifier) as a gate specifically layered on top of an existing N-of-M technical-confirmation checklist (i.e., the exact combination a document using both mechanisms together would want evidence for) — all ADX-filter sources found discuss it as a standalone filter on a simpler trend/momentum signal, not as an additional gate on an already-gated confirmation-count system.
- Quantpedia itself (named in the suggested-sources list) did not surface directly in these searches; a more targeted search of quantpedia.com specifically (rather than general "regime switching trend following" queries) would likely be needed to find any of their specific backtested regime-filter studies, if they have published any.

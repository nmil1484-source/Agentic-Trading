# Portfolio-Level Risk Management for Multi-Asset-Class Systematic Trading (Retail Scale)

## What do practitioners/literature recommend for an aggregate "total open risk"/"portfolio heat" ceiling as % of equity, and is 6% reasonable?

### Takeaway
6% of equity as an aggregate open-risk ("portfolio heat") ceiling sits almost exactly on the most widely-cited retail convention — Alexander Elder's "6% rule" — and is within the range (4-10%) other retail risk-management sources converge on. It is neither obviously too loose nor too tight; if anything it is on the tighter/more conservative end once you account for the fact that Elder's 6% is a *monthly* loss-plus-open-risk budget, while the setup being benchmarked treats 6% as a *standing, continuously-enforced, at-all-times* ceiling (a stricter application of the same number).

### Cited Findings
- Alexander Elder's "6% rule" (from *Trading for a Living* / *Come Into My Trading Room*): "Total risk on all open positions should be restricted to 6% of capital," and separately, "if net losses reach 6.0% in one month, stop trading or lock in profits." — [Incredible Charts: Alexander Elder's 6 Percent Rule](https://www.incrediblecharts.com/trading/6_percent_rule.php)
- Elder's own framing pairs the 2% per-trade rule ("shark bites" — one big loss) with the 6% total/monthly rule ("piranhas" — many small losses adding up) as the "two pillars of money management." — [Moneylife: Book Review of 'The New Trading for a Living'](https://moneylife.in/article/book-review-of-the-new-trading-for-a-living/43262.html)
- Elder's 6% heat calculation method: take 6% of capital at last month-end; for each open position, compute (month-end price − stop level) per share, add an allowance for brokerage/slippage (≥0.5%), multiply by shares held, and sum across all positions; if the sum exceeds the 6% figure, tighten stops to bring it back under. — [Incredible Charts](https://www.incrediblecharts.com/trading/6_percent_rule.php)
- TD Ameritrade's Tickertape (describing the 2% and 6% rules): "No new trades will be opened for the remainder of the month" once monthly losses plus open-trade risk hit 6% of total account equity; separately, the 2% rule risk is "the distance from entry to stop, not the trade's total size." — [TD Ameritrade / Tickertape: Rules to Manage Risk](https://tickertape.tdameritrade.com/trading/rules-to-manage-risk-15908)
- A critique of the 6% rule, from a professional-trading commentary: it is "sensible for a beginner" but may be too restrictive for a professional, since "it is entirely possible to have three losing trades in a row and this could put the 6% loss within the first few days of the month." — [Financial Wisdom TV](https://www.financialwisdomtv.com/post/trading-for-a-living)
- Other retail/educational sources on total "portfolio heat" caps vary considerably: one swing-trading calculator suggests 4-8% of equity; Wealth-Lab's own documentation cites a much looser ~20-25% ballpark; one described trading-agent configuration sets a stricter 2.0% maximum. — [LuxAlgo: Max Heat](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/max-heat.md); [Wealth-Lab Wiki: Portfolio Heat](https://www2.wealth-lab.com/WL5Wiki/psPortfolioHeat.ashx)
- CompleteTradersEdge explicitly frames "Portfolio Heat" as "The 6% Rule That Caps Your Total Open Risk," reinforcing 6% as the dominant retail-education convention for this specific metric (title/framing confirmed via search result; full article not independently fetched). — [CompleteTradersEdge: Portfolio Heat](https://completetradersedge.com/?p=231483)
- A forum-based risk framework (NexusFi) independently proposes "a per-trade limit (around 1%) plus a separate account-wide cap (for example 5%)" specifically to guard against all positions losing together — a very similar two-tier structure (per-trade % + aggregate %) to the benchmarked setup, with a slightly lower aggregate figure (5% vs. 6%). — [NexusFi: Correlation-Adjusted Position Sizing](https://nexusfi.com/a/risk-management/correlation-adjusted-position-sizing)
- Take Profit's educational piece suggests "keeping total heat around 5-10% of the account" and recommends applying a "1.3x stress multiplier" to heat totals to account for the fact that correlations and slippage both worsen in a real selloff relative to calm-market assumptions. — [Take Profit: Correlation Risk](https://takeprofitapp.com/en/learn/correlation-risk-portfolio)
- One guide frames a simpler aggregate rule as "two to three times single-trade risk" as the total-heat ceiling. — [Guy Gentile: Position Sizing](https://guygentile.com/guides/position-sizing)
- A forum streak-probability calculation (illustrative, not a validated academic study) shows why per-trade risk size matters for surviving losing streaks: at 2% risk per trade in a system with a 75% per-trade survival assumption, the probability that any given trade starts a 3-loss streak is ~42% (0.75³); at 1% risk the "locking" streak length needed is six losses, and at 0.5% it's twelve — illustrating that lower per-trade risk mechanically buys more cushion before an aggregate cap like 6% is threatened. — [BabyPips forum: Choosing Position Size](https://forums.babypips.com/t/choosing-position-size-1-2-3-4/70644/11)
- A broker education example: capping per-trade risk at 2% means a 5-consecutive-loss streak produces "a manageable drawdown of 10%." — [ICMarkets blog via search summary](https://www.icmarkets.com/blog/?p=12750)

### Inferences
- The benchmarked 6% aggregate heat cap is squarely inside the cluster of retail-education conventions (4-10%, with 6% being the single most frequently cited number via Elder). It is not an outlier in either direction.
- The benchmarked setup's *continuous, always-enforced* application of the 6% figure (checked before every new entry, at all times) is actually a stricter real-world constraint than Elder's original *monthly* 6% rule, which only resets/escalates on a calendar-month basis and allows the number to accumulate loss + open-risk together rather than open-risk alone. Treating 6% as a pure "current open-risk" ceiling (with breakeven-or-better positions counting as $0) is a more risk-averse reading of the same headline number, because it doesn't let realized losses earlier in the period "use up" the same budget as live open risk — it only measures what could still be lost right now.
- Combining a 1-3%-per-trade cap with a 6% aggregate cap mathematically limits simultaneous *fresh* (non-breakeven) open-risk exposure to roughly 2-6 concurrently-losing-capable trades at once (6% ÷ 1-3% per trade), which is consistent with the NexusFi and Guy Gentile "2-3x single-trade risk" framings found above — those sources describe caps of 2-3x a single trade's risk, i.e., effectively a 2-9% range when per-trade risk is 1-3%, bracketing the 6% figure from both sides depending on which per-trade number is in play.
- The "1.3x stress multiplier" idea (Take Profit) and the documented tendency for correlations to spike in stress (see next section) both argue that a nominal 6% heat figure, computed under calm-market correlation assumptions, likely understates true tail-scenario loss — this is a real, disclosed-worthy caveat rather than a reason the 6% figure itself is wrong.

### Gaps
- No peer-reviewed or institutional (as opposed to retail-education-blog) source was found that independently derives or validates a specific numeric "portfolio heat" ceiling; essentially all retail guidance on this specific concept traces back to, or echoes, Elder's original 6%/month figure with cosmetic variation (4-10% range). This is a genuine gap in rigor across the whole retail risk-management literature, not just this research pass — treat 6% as "industry-convention reasonable," not "empirically optimized."
- No source quantified exactly how much a 6% nominal heat cap degrades (in realized-loss terms) once stress-period correlation increases are applied — the "1.3x multiplier" is itself an unvalidated heuristic, not a backtested figure.

## How should correlation between asset classes factor into position-count or exposure caps? Is there a standard approach to concentration limits in correlated-asset baskets?

### Takeaway
There is no single codified standard, but the consistent practitioner principle is: treat a basket of correlated positions as one larger position for risk-budgeting purposes, cap the number of concurrent positions in a correlated bucket more tightly than for a diversified one, and expect correlations to rise toward 1.0 precisely when it matters most (broad risk-off). Crypto specifically is well-documented as behaving like a single macro risk asset in stress, which supports a tighter per-basket concurrent-position cap than equities (where correlation is more sector-differentiated) — exactly the asymmetry the benchmarked setup already applies (2-per-theme for equities vs. a 6-of-8-coin cap for crypto, albeit crypto's cap is comparatively loose relative to the "treat correlated assets as one trade" principle).

### Cited Findings
- "Correlations tend to rise in selloffs, so a 30% correlation in calm markets can approach 90% in a crisis." — [Take Profit: Correlation Risk in Trading Portfolios](https://takeprofitapp.com/en/learn/correlation-risk-portfolio)
- Take Profit's suggested mechanism is a "correlation discount": group correlated positions and discount their combined risk rather than simply summing nominal per-trade risk — e.g., two 1% trades in a highly correlated pair are treated as ~1.8% combined risk, not 2%, under their worked example. — [Take Profit](https://takeprofitapp.com/en/learn/correlation-risk-portfolio)
- A second practitioner source (cited secondhand, unvalidated) suggests cutting combined position size by 25-50% once pairwise correlation exceeds roughly 0.5-0.7. — [Relicus Road blog, via search summary](https://relicusroad-md-website-0hrwa.kinsta.page/blog/risk-management-advanced-indicators)
- On crypto specifically: following the October 10, 2025 tariff-driven liquidation event, CoinMetrics data showed BTC-ETH correlation rising from 0.69 to 0.73 and BTC-XRP correlation rising from 0.75 to 0.77 over the following eight days — i.e., already-high correlations rose further under stress. — [Cointelegraph: Without Bitcoin, what happens to ether and XRP](https://cointelegraph.com/news/without-bitcoin-what-happens-to-ether-and-xrp)
- The same analysis argues that in liquidity crises, altcoins "stop reflecting their individual utility" (e.g., Ethereum's transaction volume, XRP's institutional adoption) and the market instead "treats crypto as one asset class," transmitted through both a liquidity/structural channel (margin calls, forced liquidations, capital outflows hitting all crypto assets simultaneously) and a sentiment channel. — [Cointelegraph](https://cointelegraph.com/news/without-bitcoin-what-happens-to-ether-and-xrp)
- Bitcoin's annualized volatility "has regularly exceeded 60-80%," versus roughly 15-20% for the S&P 500 — a baseline volatility difference relevant to why a crypto-specific tighter stop-distance ceiling and smaller per-position cap (as used in the benchmarked setup) is appropriate even before considering correlation. — [BIT Knowledge Hub: Risk Asset](https://www.bit.com/knowledge-hub/risk-asset)
- On equity-side correlation concentration limits specifically, one forex-education source is cited as a cross-asset-class analogy: EUR/USD and GBP/USD "move together 70-85% of the time," and trading both as separate bets is "closer to leveraged concentration on a single underlying factor than to diversification" — directly analogous to why a flat per-theme/per-sector cap (not just a raw position count) is the standard concentration-limiting mechanism. — [search summary citing a trading-education source, "how many uncorrelated positions" query]
- On raw position-count diversification benchmarks (equities, not directly transferable to a correlated-asset-class setting but useful context): one source cites Elton & Gruber-style findings that diversification benefits "flatten out after roughly 20-30 holdings" for broadly uncorrelated equities; another practitioner source suggests ~10 positions is "the point at which you start to get the benefits of managing risk," while cautioning that actively managing more than 10 is operationally hard for a discretionary/short-term trader; a secondhand citation of Stan Weinstein suggests a small account ($1,000-$25,000) should hold "no more than 5 or 6" positions. — [search summary, "diversification myths / position concentration" query]
- All of the above position-count sources explicitly caveat that correlation matters more than raw count: "you have to pay attention to how uncorrelated your assets are, not just what the number of assets are." — [search summary]

### Inferences
- The benchmarked setup's structural asymmetry — a 2-concurrent-position correlation cap per *theme* for equities (where sectors/catalysts genuinely differ) versus a 6-of-8 concurrent-position cap for the *entire* crypto allowlist (where the literature shows the whole asset class correlates toward 1.0 in stress) — has the right *direction* (tighter cap for the more-correlated bucket) but the crypto number (6 of 8, i.e., 75% of the allowlist open at once) is loose relative to the "treat a correlated basket as one position" principle found across sources. If BTC/ETH/XRP/SOL/LINK/AAVE/HBAR/AVAX genuinely behave as a single macro risk factor in a risk-off move (as the Cointelegraph evidence suggests, and as the operating document's own §21 reasoning explicitly states — "crypto still behaves as a single, highly-correlated macro asset class much of the time"), then holding 6 of them simultaneously is closer to one oversized, undiversified bet than to 6 independent risk exposures, even though each individual position is sized within its own per-position cap. The 6% aggregate heat cap is the real backstop here, but heat is computed from *current stop distance*, not from a correlation-adjusted basket view — so a scenario where all 6 crypto positions gap down together in one fast move could realize a loss larger than the heat cap implies, because the heat cap's math (summing nominal planned risk) doesn't apply the "correlated-basket discount/haircut" the correlation literature recommends.
- No source gave a crypto-specific "how many of a correlated basket can you hold" number; the general forex/equity analogies (2-6 positions as the point where correlation makes additional "diversification" illusory) suggest the crypto cap would ideally sit closer to its earlier, tighter historical value (2-3 concurrent positions, as this same system used earlier in its own history) than its current 6, if judged purely against the correlation literature rather than against capital-deployment goals.

### Gaps
- No source provided a quantitative, validated formula for translating "N correlated positions, each sized at X% risk" into a single correlation-adjusted aggregate risk number (the "haircut" examples found are illustrative/anecdotal, not derived from a tested model) — this is a genuine methodological gap across the retail literature, meaning any such adjustment the operating document might adopt would be a reasonable heuristic, not an industry-standard calculation.
- No institutional-grade source was found specifically benchmarking "how many positions in a single named-allowlist crypto basket is prudent at retail scale" — all crypto-correlation evidence found is about BTC/ETH/XRP co-movement in one specific October 2025 stress event, not a general concentration-limit study.

## How should fundamentally different strategies (multi-day swing equities, same-day day-trading, continuous 24/7 crypto trend-following) share one account-wide risk budget — independent allocation vs. dynamic/performance-based allocation?

### Takeaway
The CTA/managed-futures literature's standard approach is a *risk-budget* framework: each strategy/sleeve gets an explicit target risk contribution (not a dollar allocation), sized by its own volatility so that different-horizon, different-instrument strategies contribute comparably to total portfolio risk, with a portfolio-level scaling factor hitting an overall risk target. Retail-level sources separately describe an alternative/complementary layer — equity-curve-based throttling and performance-based reallocation — that dynamically shifts weight toward better-performing strategies, though with disclosed costs (lag, return give-up). The benchmarked setup currently uses the "independent, mode-specific fixed caps" approach (not dynamic reallocation), which is a defensible baseline but is explicitly the less sophisticated of the two documented approaches.

### Cited Findings
- The standard CTA sizing formula (attributed to Kaminski, found via a CME-hosted paper): position size for a given market = (market conviction × market risk allocation) ÷ market volatility, then scaled by "a portfolio scaling factor" to hit a total portfolio risk target. — [CME Group: Quantifying CTA Risk Management](https://www.cmegroup.com/education/files/quantifying-cta-risk-Management.pdf)
- Dividing by volatility is explicitly so that "each contract contributes a similar dollar-risk to the overall portfolio" regardless of instrument — directly analogous to needing a common risk unit across equities/options/crypto rather than comparing raw dollar allocations. — [The Hedge Fund Journal: Quantifying CTA Risk Management](https://thehedgefundjournal.com/quantifying-cta-risk-management/)
- One educational description of the mechanism: contract counts scale as "capital × target volatility ÷ (contract value × realized volatility)" — so "when market volatility spikes, the algorithm automatically downsizes contract volume to keep total portfolio risk constant," and vice versa when volatility collapses. — [search summary, CTA risk budgeting query]
- A recent (2026) CFA Institute Enterprising Investor piece describes CTA trend "sleeves" that are "combined with risk-parity weights so that each sleeve is an investable, volatility-controlled portfolio" — i.e., multiple trend-following sub-strategies/horizons are blended using risk-parity-style weighting rather than fixed dollar splits. — [CFA Institute: Decoding CTA Allocations by Trend Horizon](https://blogs.cfainstitute.org/investor/2026/01/28/decoding-cta-allocations-by-trend-horizon)
- A fund-oriented write-up on multi-strategy risk budgeting warns that a book allocated purely by dollars can be "secretly dominated by one or two high-volatility strategies" — the risk-budget approach (target risk contribution per strategy, with a hard ceiling on any one strategy's share of total risk) exists specifically to prevent that. — [search summary, equity-curve/dynamic-allocation query, citing a fund-oriented source]
- A retail-specific framing (unvalidated, blog-level) suggests "most retail traders allocate 50/50 as [a] starting baseline between two strategies," moving to a 60/40 split favoring whichever strategy shows higher measured expectancy, while cautioning that "most retail traders shouldn't run multi-strategy portfolios because of operational complexity." — [search summary, "multi-strategy retail trading account risk budget" query]
- The same source stresses that strategy *pairing* matters: "other pairings often produce correlated results that don't deliver true diversification" — i.e., independent risk budgets across strategies are only genuinely additive diversification if the strategies' return streams are actually low/negatively correlated, not merely nominally "different" (different instruments or holding periods do not guarantee low correlation of P&L).

### Inferences
- The benchmarked setup's current design — each mode (Mode B swing equities, Mode C day-trading, crypto) having its own independent, fixed per-trade risk % and its own independent position-count cap, all bounded by one shared aggregate 6% heat ceiling and one shared 90%/10% deployment ceiling — is structurally similar to a *simplified, non-volatility-scaled* version of the CTA risk-budget approach: each "sleeve" has its own risk allocation, and a portfolio-level cap (the heat cap) bounds the total. What it lacks, relative to the CTA standard, is (a) explicit volatility-normalization of per-trade risk across modes (the document uses flat % of equity per mode rather than inversely scaling by each mode's/instrument's realized volatility), and (b) any correlation term between the three sleeves' own P&L streams — the heat cap only sums nominal planned-risk dollars, not a risk-parity-style contribution that accounts for how often Mode B, Mode C, and crypto's returns move together or apart.
- Given that Mode C (day-trading) and crypto (24/7) overlap in market hours with Mode B (swing) and each other, and all three draw from the same account-wide 6% heat and 90% deployment ceilings, the "independent per-mode risk allocation bounded by one shared aggregate cap" design is a reasonable, retail-appropriate approximation of the institutional risk-budget principle — it is simpler than true volatility/correlation-scaled risk parity, but that added sophistication has real implementation cost (continuously re-estimating each sleeve's volatility and cross-sleeve correlation) that is arguably not justified at this account's scale (low thousands of dollars) given the "retail traders shouldn't run multi-strategy portfolios because of operational complexity" caveat found above.

### Gaps
- No source directly addressed the specific combination this system runs (swing equities + intraday day-trading + 24/7 crypto trend-following, same account, same aggregate cap) — all CTA/risk-budget material is about multi-market futures trend-following sleeves, and the retail multi-strategy material is thin and largely unvalidated (blog-level, not backtested). Any mapping from "CTA sleeve risk budgeting" onto "Mode A/B/C + crypto in one retail account" is an analogy, not a directly transferable, validated practice.
- No source quantified how much, if any, volatility-normalization (vs. flat % allocation) would have changed historical outcomes for a small multi-strategy retail account specifically — this would require a dedicated backtest, not available from this search pass.

## What documented approaches exist for dynamically reducing allocation to an underperforming strategy/asset class (vs. a fixed static split) — equity curve trading, Kelly-based allocation, performance-based sizing?

### Takeaway
Equity-curve-based throttling (sizing a strategy's positions off its own trailing P&L curve) and performance-based reallocation (shifting weight toward strategies with better trailing risk-adjusted metrics, often Sortino ratio) are both documented, named retail/practitioner techniques for dynamic allocation — as is Kelly-criterion-based allocation across multiple simultaneous bets, which has a real but computationally-heavier academic literature. All three carry a disclosed, consistent tradeoff: smoothing the equity curve and controlling drawdown comes at the cost of giving up some total return, because de-risking a struggling strategy and re-engaging it both lag the strategy's own actual turning points.

### Cited Findings
- "Equity-curve-based throttling" is explicitly named and described: the strategy's own P&L line drives its position size — "traders may reduce size, pause trading, or restore size based on rules applied to that curve." It is explicitly distinguished from (but often combined with) performance-based reallocation *across* multiple systems in one account. — [LuxAlgo: Equity-curve-based Throttling](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/equity-curve-based-throttling.md)
- The same source reports that "studies and backtests tend to show it smooths the curve and shortens drawdowns while giving up some total return, because re-entry always lags recovery," and that common lookback windows for the equity-curve moving average run "from 10 to 50 closed trades, with no canonical setting." — [LuxAlgo: Equity-curve-based Throttling](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/equity-curve-based-throttling/)
- A separate practitioner description of cross-strategy dynamic allocation: "adjusting exposure based on performance — scaling up well-performing strategies and reducing exposure to underperformers," scoring strategies on trailing metrics and "favoring the Sortino ratio over the Sharpe ratio because it penalizes only downside volatility." — [What Works in Trading (Substack): My thoughts on how to run multiple [strategies]](https://whatworksintrading.substack.com/p/my-thoughts-on-how-to-run-multiple)
- An implementation-level reference (RustyBT docs) lists multiple named allocation algorithms available for multi-strategy portfolios: "fixed, dynamic, risk parity, and Kelly-criterion allocation" — confirming Kelly-based allocation is a recognized, implementable option alongside risk-parity and simple dynamic-performance weighting, not solely a theoretical construct. — [RustyBT docs: Allocation Algorithms](https://rustybt.readthedocs.io/en/latest/api/portfolio-management/allocation-algorithms/)
- On Kelly specifically extended to multiple simultaneous bets/strategies: an arXiv paper models L parallel investments with a fraction allocated to each, evaluating joint (not independent) outcome probabilities across all bets together, with the remainder held as cash. — [arXiv:1806.05293, Generalized framework for applying the Kelly criterion to stock markets](https://arxiv.org/pdf/1806.05293)
- A cited academic result: an investor placing several simultaneous Kelly-style bets does better than one placing a single bet (diversification benefit within the Kelly framework itself). — [arXiv:2402.15588, Sizing the bets in a focused portfolio](https://arxiv.org/pdf/2402.15588)
- A practical caveat on joint-outcome Kelly: "the optimal Kelly strategy generally needs numerical optimization over a joint outcome space... scales exponentially with the number of bets" for a naive implementation, "limit[ing] existing methods to small problems" — relevant since this is a real computational/practical barrier to applying true multi-bet Kelly outside of a quant-research context. — [Bloomberg/Cornell seminar abstract, cited via search summary](https://www.duffield.cornell.edu/events/cfem-and-ubs-ai-data-research-seminar-with-ruslan-tepelyan-bloomberg/)
- A separate 2025 paper (Hakobyan & Lototsky) flags that full Kelly sizing, despite being long-run growth-optimal, "can be quite aggressive" — the standard practitioner response (not from this specific paper, but consistent with broader Kelly literature) is to run "fractional Kelly" at well below the mathematically optimal fraction to control volatility of outcomes. — [arXiv:2503.17927](https://www.arxiv.org/pdf/2503.17927)
- On drawdown-triggered de-risking generally (not equity-curve-specific): one commentary frames the core principle as "sizing against volatility keeps effective risk stable," making "the worst losing streak survivable by design rather than by luck" — i.e., the preferred lever for retail risk control is sizing discipline up front, with equity-curve throttling as a secondary, reactive layer on top. — [search summary, "drawdown based position size reduction" query]

### Inferences
- The benchmarked setup does not currently implement any dynamic/performance-based reallocation across its three sleeves (Mode B, Mode C, crypto) — each has a static, fixed risk/position-count allocation regardless of trailing performance. Given the operating document's own logged profit-factor tally (crypto's combined profit factor well below 1.0, i.e., net-losing, versus Mode B's profit factor above 1.0 per the document's own change log), this is precisely the situation the equity-curve-throttling and performance-based-reallocation literature is designed to address: a mechanical rule that reduces crypto's position-count cap, per-trade risk %, or both while its trailing profit factor stays under some threshold (and restores them once it recovers) would be a direct, literature-supported application of equity-curve throttling — at the disclosed cost of some lag (both in cutting exposure after the edge has already degraded, and in restoring exposure after it's already recovered).
- A full joint-outcome Kelly-based allocation across all three sleeves is almost certainly disproportionate engineering effort for this account's scale — the literature itself flags that true multi-bet Kelly requires numerical optimization over a combinatorial outcome space and is typically only tractable for small numbers of well-characterized bets in a quant-research setting, not a fast-moving, qualitatively-gated discretionary-signal system like this one. A simpler, documented alternative — equity-curve throttling per sleeve, using trailing win-rate/profit-factor exactly as the operating document already tracks in its §16 item 16 tally — is the more proportionate, literature-supported next step if dynamic allocation is desired, rather than a Kelly-optimization layer.

### Gaps
- No source provided a specific, validated rule (e.g., "cut size by X% after Y consecutive losing trades" or "halve allocation once trailing profit factor drops below Z over N trades") that could be lifted directly into an operating document — all equity-curve-throttling guidance found is qualitative/structural ("common lookback windows run 10-50 trades") rather than a single codified threshold. Any specific threshold adopted would be the operating document's own calibration choice, not an externally validated number.
- No source specifically addressed combining equity-curve throttling *across asset classes with different trade frequencies* (e.g., a 24/7 crypto sleeve generating far more closed trades per week than a 2-15-day swing-equity sleeve) — the "10 to 50 closed trades" lookback convention found doesn't address how to make such a window comparable in calendar time across sleeves with very different trading cadences.

## Overall verdict on the benchmarked setup (6% aggregate heat / 1-3% per-trade / 2-correlated-position caps)

### Takeaway
On the specific numbers asked about, the benchmarked setup is in a reasonable, not-obviously-miscalibrated range relative to retail risk-management convention, with one clear strength (the aggregate heat cap is close to the most commonly cited number, and the per-trade risk tiers 1-3%/6-12%-of-premium/1.5% are each individually within normal retail ranges) and one clear, literature-supported soft spot (the crypto concurrent-position cap, at 6 of 8 allowlisted coins, is loose relative to how correlated the literature shows crypto becomes under stress — tighter than equities' 2-per-theme cap in spirit, but not tight enough in practice given that "crypto behaves as one correlated macro asset" is explicitly acknowledged in the operating document's own reasoning).

### Inferences
- **6% aggregate heat cap: reasonable, not too loose or too tight.** It matches the dominant retail convention (Elder's 6% rule) and sits inside the broader cited range (4-10%); applying it continuously (rather than monthly, as Elder's original) is a stricter, more conservative implementation of the same number, not a looser one.
- **1-3% per-trade equity risk: reasonable.** This matches the widely-cited "1-2% per trade" professional convention, with the top of the range (3%, used for Mode B's current setting per the operating document) at the high end of what's typically recommended but not outside it; the key caveat from the literature is that *survivability of a losing streak* degrades meaningfully as per-trade risk rises from 1% toward 3% (the streak-probability illustrations above), so 3% narrows the cushion before the aggregate 6% cap is threatened by ordinary variance, not just a true tail event.
- **Options/LEAPS sized at 6-12% of equity (premium-at-risk, not stop-distance risk): structurally different from the equity/crypto risk-per-trade numbers and not directly comparable to the 1-3% figures** — because these are maximum-loss-of-premium caps (long options can lose 100% of premium), a 12% cap is a materially larger single-trade tail-risk figure in relative terms than any of the stop-distance-based caps elsewhere in the system. No source specifically validated a "% of equity in options premium" convention, so this can't be benchmarked against established practice directly — it is best understood as its own, separately-reasoned sizing ceiling rather than one derived from the same framework as the per-trade equity/crypto risk caps.
- **2-correlated-position cap for equities: reasonable and consistent with concentration-limit literature** (the 2-6 position range found across sources for "correlated basket" caps).
- **6-of-8 concurrent-position cap for crypto: the one number in this set that sits furthest from what the correlation literature would suggest.** Given the documented tendency of crypto to behave as a single correlated macro factor in risk-off conditions (correlations rising further, not weakening, exactly when it matters), a tighter concurrent-position cap (closer to 2-3, which is in fact where this same system's own cap started before being loosened) would be better aligned with the "treat a correlated basket as one trade" principle than the current 6. The aggregate 6% heat cap is a real backstop, but it sums nominal planned risk without any correlation-based haircut, so it does not fully substitute for a tighter position-count cap in a basket this correlated.
- **No dynamic/performance-based reallocation between sleeves is currently used**, which is a gap relative to the most sophisticated layer of best practice found (equity-curve throttling, performance-based reallocation) — though adopting it is a deliberate choice to make (added complexity, lag cost) rather than an obvious must-fix, especially at this account's retail scale where the literature itself cautions that multi-strategy allocation adds real operational complexity.

### Gaps
- All verdicts above are benchmarked against retail-education convention and CTA-style institutional principles found via search, not against a backtest of this specific system's own trade history — a genuinely rigorous verdict would also incorporate the system's own realized volatility/correlation across its three sleeves, which was outside the scope of this web-research pass.

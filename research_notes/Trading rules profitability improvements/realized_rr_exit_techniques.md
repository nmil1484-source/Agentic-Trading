# Improving Realized Reward-to-Risk Beyond a Single Fixed-Target Computation

## What is "scaling out" / partial profit-taking, and does it improve expectancy vs. a single all-or-nothing target or a single trailing stop?

### Takeaway
Scaling out (selling a fraction of the position at an initial target, trailing the remainder) reliably raises the *percentage of trades that close green* and smooths equity, but there is no rigorous backtested evidence that it raises average expectancy/realized R for a trend-following system — several sources (including a magazine-cited backtest reference that could not be independently verified) argue it structurally *reduces* average profit per trade versus a single full-position exit, because it systematically trims size off the exact trades (the big trend winners) that a low-win-rate trend system depends on. The honest framing is: scaling out trades average-R for psychological smoothing and win-rate optics, and whether it helps *total* expectancy depends entirely on how often price runs past the first target in that specific system — something that must be measured empirically, not assumed.

### Cited Findings
- A scaling-out concept reference states plainly that scaling out "does not inherently increase profits" — it typically raises the fraction of trades that end green but caps the size of the largest winners; net expectancy effect depends on how often price runs past the first target, and the source advises measuring this in the trader's own history rather than assuming a benefit — [LuxAlgo: Scaling Out](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/scaling-out.md)
- A retail-education piece argues scaling out is "the most popular retail exit method" and simultaneously "the most over-recommended," contending it mainly helps when outcomes are bimodal and the trader needs psychological smoothing rather than when it improves the underlying math — [Trader's Second Brain: Take-Profit Methods](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/take-profit-methods)
- A trading-tools article references simulations reportedly published by Howard Bandy in *Active Trader* magazine showing scaling out reduces average profit per trade versus a single full-position target — this specific claim could **not** be independently verified from the search results and should be treated as unconfirmed pending a primary source — [Optimus Futures: A Better Way to Scale Into and Out of Positions](https://optimusfutures.com/blog/a-better-way-to-scale-into-and-out-of-positions/)
- A critical forum-sourced view argues that multi-contract scaling out amounts to "reverse position sizing" — leaving the largest size on the losing/small-winning trades and the smallest size on the biggest winners, which is the opposite of what a system wanting to capture trend tails wants — cited via [EliteTrader: Why Scale Out](https://elitetrader.com/et/threads/why-scale-out.147077/) (reported secondhand through search aggregation; original thread not directly fetched)
- Comparative math cited by a take-profit-methods guide: a 50%-at-1R / 50%-at-2R scale-out produces a blended average of 1.5R on that trade, versus a consistent, undiluted R-multiple from a full exit at whichever single target is chosen — illustrating mechanically why scaled exits blend outcomes rather than preserve the tail — [Trader's Second Brain: Take-Profit Methods](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/take-profit-methods)
- For trend-following specifically, one source notes the strategy's edge depends on the occasional large winner running as long as the trend persists (variable holding time); a partial-exit ladder "can cut into that tail" by locking in a fraction before the biggest moves fully develop — [Optimus Futures: A Better Way to Scale Into and Out of Positions](https://optimusfutures.com/blog/a-better-way-to-scale-into-and-out-of-positions/)
- A practical backtesting caveat: on at least one retail platform (TradeZella), multiple profit targets/stops are not natively supported in backtesting, so partial exits are simulated by hand after the fact and R-multiples computed retroactively — meaning many "scaling out works" claims online are not from rigorous systematic backtests at all — [TradeZella: How to Scale In/Out or Take Partials in Backtesting](https://help.tradezella.com/en/articles/10395552-how-to-scale-in-out-of-a-position-or-take-partials-in-backtesting-for-stocks-and-futures)
- A NinjaTrader forum thread reports that market-order scale-outs can work cleanly in backtest but not match in real-time execution, meaning backtested partial-exit results can look better than what live trading would actually deliver — [NinjaTrader Forum: Taking Partial Profits](https://forum.ninjatrader.com/forum/ninjatrader-7/strategy-development-aa/63570-taking-partial-profits)
- Van Tharp's R-multiple framework defines system expectancy as the average R-multiple across trades, but there is no documented, authoritative Tharp-published method for how scaled-out trades should be scored into that average R calculation — a forum user raising the identical question got no definitive answer in the material found — [NexusFi forum thread](https://nexusfi.com/showthread.php?p=103706); [CrossTrade: R-Multiple](https://crosstrade.io/learn/performance-metrics/r-multiple)

### Inferences
- Because scaling out mechanically blends a locked-in smaller-R outcome with a larger/trailed-remainder outcome, it necessarily *reduces the variance* of trade outcomes — which is exactly the opposite of what a trend/momentum system with a low win rate needs, since that system's positive expectancy is concentrated in the right tail (a few large winners). This is a structural tension, not a design error, and must be weighed deliberately rather than adopted by default.
- The "does it help" answer is genuinely empirical and system-specific — no source found here supplies a general backtested verdict that generalizes across systems; the correct test is a same-system, same-trade-sample replay comparing (a) full exit at the fixed target, (b) full trailing stop with no fixed target, and (c) a specific partial-exit ladder, measured in realized average R, not win rate.

### Gaps
- No primary-source, quantified, peer-reviewed or rigorously-documented backtest was found that directly measures scaling-out's effect on *average realized R* for a momentum/breakout system specifically (as opposed to win rate or smoothness). The Bandy/Active Trader reference surfaced only secondhand and could not be verified — flagged explicitly as unconfirmed.
- No documented method was found for how Van Tharp's own expectancy framework treats partial exits mathematically (e.g., whether to weight by share count or treat as independent R events) — this is a real gap in the literature search, not just this note.

## What are "measured move" and Fibonacci-extension price targets, and how reliable are they versus simpler methods (prior swing high, round numbers)?

### Takeaway
Both measured-move and Fibonacci-extension targets are *consensus-watched reference levels derived from prior price geometry*, not validated forecasting tools — every source found frames them as "where traders expect to take profit" (a self-fulfilling, crowd-behavior rationale) rather than as backtested predictive levels, and no quantified hit-rate/reliability statistic comparing them to simpler methods (prior swing high, round numbers) was found anywhere in this search.

### Cited Findings
- Measured-move definition: projects the length of a prior trend leg forward from the end of a corrective pullback (point-distance), with a percentage variant (projecting the ratio rather than the absolute point distance) recommended for log-scale charts and large moves — [LuxAlgo: Measured Move](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/measured-move.md)
- The same source states a target carries more weight when it coincides with a prior price level *or* a Fibonacci extension than when either tool is used alone — i.e., confluence is explicitly treated as the thing that increases confidence, not either method standing alone — [LuxAlgo: Measured Move](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/measured-move.md)
- Fibonacci extensions are described as "widely watched reference levels, not forecasts"; once price clears the anchored swing's extreme, the 1.272 and 1.618 extensions are commonly used as first and second staged profit-taking objectives — [LuxAlgo: Fib Extension](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/fib-extension.md)
- The measured-move and Fibonacci-extension techniques overlap heavily in practice: a classic 1:1 measured move is itself one specific ratio, and many traders substitute 0.618 or 1.272 multiples of the first leg instead of a literal 1:1 projection — blurring any clean distinction between "measured move" and "Fib extension" as separate methods — [LuxAlgo: Measured Move](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/measured-move.md)
- The only reliability "evidence" surfaced was a single anecdotal S&P 500 case study where price paused for several days at a 161.8% Fibonacci extension — explicitly one example, not a sample or statistic — [Market Masters: Two Strategies For Identifying Price Targets](https://seeitmarket.com/?p=17869)
- A forum critique argued a specific practitioner's measured-move derivation was methodologically flawed on a particular chart — one opinion, not a dataset — found via search aggregation, original source not independently re-verified.

### Inferences
- Because every source frames these levels as "commonly watched" rather than "empirically validated," their practical value is best understood as behavioral/self-fulfilling (many market participants place orders near the same levels, which can create real support/resistance effects) rather than as a geometrically "true" predictive target. This is consistent with how technical analysis levels generally function.
- Given the acknowledged overlap between measured-move and Fib-extension methods, and the explicit "confluence increases confidence" framing, the more defensible practice is to require **multiple independent target methods to cluster** (e.g., a measured move AND a round number AND a prior high all landing near the same price) rather than relying on any single projection method as the sole target — this mirrors the existing CLAUDE.md §13.E "confluence note" already in place for entry confirmations, extended to exits.
- No source supports treating a Fibonacci extension or measured move as more reliable than a simple prior-swing-high or round-number target; nor was evidence found supporting the reverse. The honest conclusion is these methods are **unranked by rigor** in the available literature — all are heuristic.

### Gaps
- No backtested hit-rate or reliability statistic was found anywhere comparing measured-move targets, Fibonacci extensions, prior-swing-high targets, and round-number targets head-to-head. This is a genuine, stated gap — the key question as posed ("how reliable are they considered versus simpler methods") could not be answered quantitatively from available sources; only consensus/practitioner framing was available.

## What trailing-stop methodologies let winners run further than a fixed-percentage trailing stop, and what's the tradeoff?

### Takeaway
The Chandelier Exit (Chuck LeBeau's ATR-multiple-off-the-highest-high trailing stop, popularized by Alexander Elder) and trailing under a moving average (e.g., rising 20-EMA) are the two most documented volatility/trend-adaptive trailing methods that let winners run further than a fixed-percentage trail; the universal, well-documented tradeoff is that a wider/looser trail (bigger ATR multiple, slower moving average) captures more of a sustained trend but gives back more open profit before exiting, while a tighter trail locks in more profit but gets whipsawed out of trends earlier and more often. One academic study on U.S. equities (1926–2016) found trailing stops reduce risk/tail-loss relative to buy-and-hold but do **not** clearly improve raw or risk-adjusted (Sharpe) returns, and their edge erodes further once transaction costs and turnover are accounted for — a materially different claim than "trailing stops increase average realized R."

### Cited Findings
- **Chandelier Exit formula and origin:** Standard parameters are a 22-period lookback and 3× ATR multiplier; for longs the stop = highest high − 3×ATR(22). Most sources credit Chuck LeBeau as originator (with one source dating his System Traders Club bulletins to the late 1990s, another tying the concept to *Computer Analysis of the Futures Market*, 1992), with Alexander Elder popularizing it in his 2002 book *Come Into My Trading Room* — [Barchart: Chandelier Exit](https://www.barchart.com/education/technical-indicators/chandelier-exit); [StockCharts ChartSchool: Chandelier Exit](https://chartschool.stockcharts.com/table-of-contents/technical-indicators-and-overlays/technical-overlays/chandelier-exit)
- The stop is designed to only ratchet upward on new highs and not fall back during pullbacks; one source notes some implementations explicitly add a ratchet/floor mechanism to enforce this, because a naive formula (rising ATR with no new high) can otherwise mathematically lower the stop — [LuxAlgo: Chandelier Stop](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/chandelier-stop.md)
- Sources disagree on the precise anchor: one says the stop trails the highest high over a fixed rolling lookback window; another says it trails the highest high (or highest close) *since trade entry* specifically — this is a real implementation detail that changes behavior and must be fixed deliberately (trade-entry anchoring is generally what trend-following position management intends) — flagged explicitly as a documented conflicting detail across sources reviewed via [LuxAlgo: Chandelier Stop](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/chandelier-stop.md)
- Trailing under a rising moving average (e.g., a 200-EMA trend filter combined with an ATR trail) is documented as a common combination specifically to avoid false trailing-stop signals in choppy, range-bound conditions — the trend filter's own authors state that without it, the system "will generate signals in both directions during choppy, range-bound conditions" — [TradingView: ATR Trailing Stop Strategy with EMA Trend Filter](https://www.tradingview.com/script/43eIQfDj-ATR-Trailing-Stop-Strategy-with-EMA-Trend-Filter/) (vendor script documentation, not independently backtested data)
- Direct tradeoff statement: "ATR trailing stop still fails in a sideways-moving market" even with its volatility adaptation — [TradingView: ATR Trailing Stop advance script notes]
- **Academic quantified study** (Dai, Marshall, Nguyen & Visaltanachoti, 2019; 25,997 U.S. stocks, July 1926–December 2016, gross basis, testing 1%/5%/10%/20% trailing thresholds): trailing-stop portfolios had both lower average gross returns AND lower volatility than buy-and-hold; gross Sharpe ratios were mostly *lower*, not higher, for the trailing-stop strategies — the benefit shows up only under a certainty-equivalent utility model accounting for investor risk aversion (i.e., trailing stops can be utility-improving for a risk-averse investor even while *not* raising the Sharpe ratio), and primarily through reduced tail risk (lower value-at-risk/expected shortfall), concentrated in stocks that were eventually delisted, in volatile/liquid/low-book-to-market names, and in declining markets — [summarized via CXO Advisory review](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=32408) of the original study at [acfr.aut.ac.nz](https://acfr.aut.ac.nz/research/using-trailing-stop-loss-rules-to-reduce-risk) (original paper page returned HTTP 403 to direct fetch; relied on the CXO Advisory secondary summary, which names the authors, sample, and period explicitly)
- The same review states trailing stops "outperform traditional price-based or time-based stops" in their comparison, but **no quantified head-to-head number was given** for that specific claim — it is qualitative in the available summary — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=32408)
- Cost/turnover caveat, stated explicitly in the same review: all results above are **gross** (pre-transaction-cost); tighter trailing thresholds generate very high turnover, and liquidity can dry up during stop-outs (raising realized trading costs); the review goes so far as to suggest a simple moving-average rule on a broad index might achieve similar downside protection "with far less tracking and trading" than name-by-name trailing stops — [CXO Advisory](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=32408)
- A separate, apparently informal summary (Quant-Investing blog, citing an OMX Stockholm 30 study, 1998–2009) reports the 20% trailing level had the highest average quarterly return and the 15% level the highest cumulative return in that sample, with only the tightest (5%) level underperforming buy-and-hold — directionally consistent with "looser trail keeps more of the trend" but this is a single-market, single-study, secondary-sourced claim not independently verified here — [Quant-Investing: Truths About Stop-Losses](https://www.quant-investing.com/blog/truths-about-stop-losses-that-nobody-wants-to-believe)
- General mechanism-level tradeoff, stated consistently across multiple practitioner sources: a trailing stop reshapes the outcome distribution rather than adding edge outright — fewer large winners "round-trip" back to breakeven/loss, but more trades get stopped out on ordinary noise before a trend even develops, and the net effect depends on the specific trail parameters and must be backtested on the system's own instrument/timeframe — [Optimus Futures: Fixed Targets vs. Trailing Stop](https://optimusfutures.com/blog/?p=3837)

### Inferences
- The clearest, most defensible generalization supported by the sources: wider/slower trailing mechanisms (bigger ATR multiple, slower moving average, Chandelier-style anchor off the peak-since-entry) systematically capture more of a sustained trend's total move at the cost of giving back a larger fraction of open profit if/when the trend reverses sharply — this is a dial, not a binary choice, and the "right" setting is a function of how much given-back profit the system's risk tolerance can absorb per trade.
- The one rigorous, quantified academic study found (Dai et al., via CXO Advisory) is sobering: it should discourage treating "add a trailing stop" as an automatic realized-R improvement. Its benefit is real but narrower than commonly assumed (tail-risk reduction and risk-averse-investor utility, not raw-return or Sharpe improvement), and it was measured on passive equity portfolios, not a momentum/breakout entry system specifically — so it is suggestive, not a direct proof, for this system's use case.
- Combining a Chandelier-style ATR trail with a moving-average confirmation (trail only applies/tightens once price loses a key EMA, or vice versa) is a documented practitioner pattern aimed at capturing the "let winners run in trends" benefit while limiting the "whipsawed in chop" cost — but no quantified backtest of that specific combination was found.

### Gaps
- No rigorous backtest was found that isolates "Chandelier Exit" specifically (as opposed to generic trailing stops) and quantifies win rate, average realized R, or total return versus a fixed-percentage trail on momentum/breakout entries specifically (as opposed to the equity buy-and-hold universe studied by Dai et al.).
- No source quantified the tradeoff curve (e.g., "a 3×ATR trail captures X% more of trend profit than a 1×ATR trail but gives back Y% more open profit on reversals") — this remains a parameter that would need to be backtested on the specific instrument/timeframe in question (watchlist equities/crypto on daily/hourly bars) rather than assumed from general literature.

## Is pyramiding/scaling INTO a winning position a documented way to raise average realized R of winning trades, and what are the risks/best practices (e.g., Turtle "N-unit" rules)?

### Takeaway
Pyramiding (the Turtle Traders' documented N-unit add-on system, among others) is a well-documented, named technique specifically for compounding size into a confirmed trend to raise a winning trade's total realized R — but it mechanically increases the position's effective risk and drawdown potential at the moment of a reversal, and the Turtle system's own discipline (each add-on raises the stop for the *entire* position, not just the new unit) is the load-bearing risk control that makes it survivable; without that discipline, pyramiding degrades into the exact same error as averaging down, applied to winners instead of losers.

### Cited Findings
- Turtle Trader N-unit mechanics, sourced from a detailed implementation referencing Curtis Faith's *Way of the Turtle* (2003, the book that made the previously-secret rules public): the Turtles used a 20-day ATR-based volatility measure ("N") and added units as the trade moved favorably, with a 2N initial stop; critically, once a second unit was added, the stop for *both* units was moved up together to the new unit's 2N stop level — i.e., pyramiding and tightening the aggregate stop happened in lockstep — [MQL5: Automating Classic Market Methods — The Original Turtle Trading Rules](https://www.mql5.com/en/articles/23448)
- Sources found **disagree on the exact add-on interval and unit cap** and this could not be resolved from the search results: one source states units were added every 0.5N of favorable movement up to a maximum of 4 units; another states units were added at full 1N intervals up to a maximum of 5 units. This discrepancy is flagged explicitly rather than resolved — a direct read of Faith's book or his original published rules would be needed to settle it — [MQL5 article](https://www.mql5.com/en/articles/23448) (0.5N/4-unit claim) vs. other aggregated search results (1N/5-unit claim; exact secondary source for this figure not independently re-confirmed)
- Position sizing was tied to N directly: size was set so that a $1 adverse move in N translated to a fixed dollar loss at the account's risk budget (the documented Turtle example: $1,000 loss on a $100,000 account for a $1 move in N) — this is the mechanism that keeps each *additional* unit's risk comparable to the first, rather than letting position size balloon arbitrarily as adds accumulate — [MQL5 article](https://www.mql5.com/en/articles/23448)
- General risk framing, consistent across multiple practitioner sources: pyramiding "converts open profit into fresh risk" because each add-on pulls the position's average cost basis toward the current (higher) price, meaning a given percentage reversal does more dollar damage to the blended position than it would have to the original single unit — disciplined implementations respond by trailing the stop for the *whole* position every time a new unit is added, exactly mirroring the Turtle approach — [Curved Trading: Adding to Winners (Pyramiding)](https://curvedtrading.com/articles/en/how/adding-to-winners-pyramiding/)
- A reversal after pyramiding can erase accumulated gains or produce a larger-than-expected drawdown, especially in volatile markets — stated as the primary risk by multiple sources — [FXOpen: What Is a Pyramiding Strategy](https://fxopen.com/blog/en/what-is-a-pyramiding-strategy-and-how-does-it-work-in-trading/amp/)
- Documented mitigations/best practices across sources: (1) shrink each successive add-on's size so later units carry proportionally less weight (consistent with the Turtle N-unit sizing logic above); (2) apply an aggregate loss cap across the whole pyramided position, not just the original unit — [Curved Trading](https://curvedtrading.com/articles/en/how/adding-to-winners-pyramiding/); [general pyramiding guides aggregated via search]
- Explicit warning that pyramiding, done without discipline, can functionally become averaging-up-into-a-reversal and "turn a winner into a loser if the trend reverses" — the same source states it suits only traders who already manage single-entry sizing and stops well, i.e., it is an addition to a sound base system, not a substitute for one — [Curved Trading: Adding to Winners (Pyramiding)](https://curvedtrading.com/articles/en/how/adding-to-winners-pyramiding/)

### Inferences
- The Turtle system is the strongest, most specific documented precedent for pyramiding as a deliberate realized-R-enhancement technique (as distinct from vague "add to winners" advice) precisely because it couples the add-on trigger (0.5N or 1N favorable move — exact figure disputed in sources found) to a simultaneous, mechanical tightening of the stop for the entire combined position. The risk control and the size-adding are not separable in the documented version; adopting the add-on rule without the matching stop-tightening rule would not be a faithful implementation and would carry materially more risk than the original system.
- Because every source frames pyramiding's core risk as "a reversal does more damage to the blended position than to the original unit," any adoption of pyramiding into an existing system should, at minimum, (a) cap total position size including all adds at the same hard per-position dollar/percentage ceiling already used for a single-unit entry (so pyramiding redistributes risk across entry points rather than increasing total exposure), and (b) require the aggregate stop to ratchet with each add, exactly as the Turtles did — this is inference from the cited mechanism, not a separate citation.

### Gaps
- No rigorous, quantified backtest was found (here) isolating pyramiding's effect on *average realized R* versus a single-entry, single-stop baseline — all evidence found is mechanistic/descriptive (how it works, what the risk is) rather than a performance comparison with numbers attached.
- The exact, authoritative Turtle add-on interval/unit-cap figure remains unresolved between two conflicting secondary accounts (0.5N/4 units vs. 1N/5 units) — this is a direct, stated gap; Curtis Faith's *Way of the Turtle* itself was not accessed in this search and would be the primary source to resolve it.

## For a system with a minimum entry R:R (e.g., 1.5:1) based on a single fixed target, would a partial-profit-taking approach (e.g., 1/3 at 1R, 1/3 at original target, trail the last 1/3 with no fixed target) measurably change realized average R:R versus pure single-target exits? Cite studies/backtests if available, or explain the mechanism if hard data isn't available.

### Takeaway
No study or backtest was found that directly tests this exact three-way split (1/3 at 1R, 1/3 at the original target, trail the last 1/3) against a pure single-target exit — this appears to be a genuine evidentiary gap in the publicly available material surfaced by this search, not just an absence in the sources chosen. What the available evidence does support is a clear mechanism-level explanation of *how and why* it would change realized average R:R, plus directionally relevant (but not identical) findings from adjacent literature: scaling out generally raises win rate while capping the largest winners (net expectancy effect ambiguous and system-dependent, per the scaling-out findings above), while leaving a trailed, no-fixed-target "runner" portion is the mechanism documented (Chandelier Exit, trailing-under-EMA) to be the standard way of letting the right tail of winning trades contribute disproportionately to a trend/momentum system's overall expectancy.

### Cited Findings
- Mechanical math for a two-way split (50% at 1R / 50% at 2R) blends to an average 1.5R on that trade, as already cited above — the same blending logic extends to a three-way split: a 1/3-at-1R / 1/3-at-original-target(e.g. 1.5R) / 1/3-trailed-with-no-cap structure produces a weighted average of {1R, 1.5R, and whatever R the trailed runner achieves} for every winning trade, meaning the realized average R on winners becomes a function of how often and how far the trailed third actually runs — this is the exact mechanism the key question asks about, confirmed structurally but not quantified for this specific split in any source found — [Trader's Second Brain: Take-Profit Methods](https://traderssecondbrain.com/guides/take-profit-methods)
- The scaling-out literature's central, repeated caveat applies directly here: "net expectancy depends on how often price runs past the first target" — for the proposed 1/3-1/3-1/3 split, this becomes "realized average R:R depends on how often, and by how much, price runs past the original fixed target" once a third of the position is freed from any fixed target and left to trail — [LuxAlgo: Scaling Out](https://www.luxalgo.com/library/concept/scaling-out.md)
- Trend-following's well-documented low-win-rate, fat-right-tail structure (one non-Covel source cites roughly 30–45% winners typical for trend-following, with profits concentrated in a small percentage of trades that become big trends) is the direct justification, found in adjacent literature, for why leaving *any* uncapped portion of a position matters disproportionately to total system expectancy — a single fixed target by construction caps every winning trade's contribution at the same R multiple, which is in tension with a return distribution where a small number of outsized winners are what make the whole system profitable — [trading dictionary entry on trend following, aggregated via search, exact publisher not independently verified](https://daytradingtoolkit.com/dictionary/trend-following)
- The only closely-related quantified academic result found (Dai et al., trailing stops on U.S. equities 1926–2016, cited above) measured trailing stops as a *complete* exit method, not as a partial-exit "runner" layered on top of a scaled structure — so while it demonstrates trailing stops' real, measurable-but-modest edge (reduced tail risk, ambiguous/negative effect on raw Sharpe, gross-basis only), it cannot be read as direct evidence for the proposed three-way hybrid split specifically — [CXO Advisory summary](https://www.cxoadvisory.com/?p=32408)

### Inferences
- Based on the confirmed blending mechanism and the scaling-out literature's own repeated caveat, the most defensible, evidence-consistent statement is: **this specific hybrid (1/3-1/3-1/3 with the final third trailed uncapped) would very likely raise the realized win rate and reduce the variance of trade outcomes (same directional effect documented for simpler scale-out structures), while its effect on average realized R is genuinely ambiguous and must be measured on the system's own trade history** — it depends entirely on the empirical distribution of how far winning trades run past the original 1.5:1 target in this specific system/universe (watchlist equities/crypto momentum setups), which no source reviewed here quantifies.
- Because the proposed structure leaves one-third of each winning trade uncapped and trailed (rather than 100% capped at a single target, or 100% uncapped with no target at all), it is a middle position between "single fixed target" and "pure trailing stop with no target" — and the literature reviewed suggests pure trailing (no target) is the mechanism most associated with capturing a trend system's outsized winners, while a single fixed target is the mechanism most associated with capping them. The proposed hybrid should be expected to sit between those two in terms of both average realized R *and* variance, roughly in proportion to the 1/3 weighting chosen — this is a structural inference from the cited blending math, not an independently sourced number.
- The most honest, evidence-grounded path to actually answering "would this measurably change realized average R:R for *our* system" is a direct historical replay: take this system's own logged trades (win/loss outcomes and, for winners, how far price ran past the originally planned 1.5:1 target before reversing) and recompute what the 1/3-1/3-1/3 structure would have realized on each, compared to the actual single-target outcome already logged. This is exactly the kind of system-specific backtest every source in this research consistently recommends over relying on general claims — and this system's `trades_log.md` already contains the raw trade outcomes needed to run that comparison.

### Gaps
- No study, backtest, or practitioner write-up was found that tests this specific 1/3-1/3-1/3 structure (or any functionally identical structure) against a pure single-target exit and reports a quantified change in realized average R:R. This is the central gap for this key question — flagged explicitly rather than inferred or estimated, per instructions not to fabricate a number.
- No data was found on the empirical frequency/magnitude distribution of "how far does price run past a 1.5:1 momentum/breakout target once it's reached" for any comparable system — this is the single most decision-relevant missing data point, and it is only answerable from this system's own historical trade data (see Inferences above), not from external literature.
